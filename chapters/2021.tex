\chapter{2021 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }David Card (Canada/USA), Joshua Angrist (USA), Guido Imbens (Netherlands/USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their empirical contributions to the analysis of causal relationships

\section{What They Did}
David Card, Joshua Angrist, and Guido Imbens advanced the empirical analysis
of causal relationships in economics through innovative methodological
contributions. Their work transformed how economists identify causal effects
from observational data, establishing new standards for empirical rigor.

David Card studied the effects of minimum wage increases on employment,
challenging the conventional wisdom that higher minimum wages reduce
employment. His research on the New Jersey-Pennsylvania minimum wage
increase found that employment actually increased, spurring extensive
research on monopsony power in labor markets. He also examined the effects
of immigration on wages and employment, finding minimal negative effects
on native workers. His work on the Mariel boatlift showed that a sudden
influx of low-skilled immigrants had no significant effect on wages of
native workers.

Angrist and Imbens made fundamental contributions to the methodology of
causal inference. They developed the concept of local average treatment
effect (LATE), showing how instrumental variables identify causal effects
for specific subpopulations. Their work clarified when and how causal
effects can be identified from observational data, providing rigorous
foundations for the use of instrumental variables in empirical research.
The LATE framework has become a standard tool in econometrics.

Card also studied the effects of education on earnings, using natural
experiments like school funding reforms and compulsory schooling laws.
His work showed that the returns to education are substantial and that
causal effects can be identified using clever research designs. Card's
research on the causal effect of class size on student achievement found
that smaller classes improve outcomes, particularly for disadvantaged
students.

Angrist's work on the Vietnam War draft lottery used the draft lottery
number as an instrument to estimate the causal effect of military service
on earnings. His research showed that military service had negative
effects on civilian earnings, challenging conventional wisdom about the
value of military experience.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Their work transformed empirical economics by providing rigorous methods
for identifying causal effects from observational data. Before their
contributions, many empirical studies relied on correlations that could
not be interpreted as causal. Their methods provided tools for distinguishing
causation from correlation.

The LATE framework has been applied to countless empirical questions, from
the effects of education on earnings to the impact of health insurance
on medical outcomes. It has become a standard tool in empirical economics,
used in journals across all social sciences. The emphasis on credible
identification has raised standards across empirical economics.

Card's empirical findings on minimum wages and immigration have influenced
policy debates worldwide. His work showed that some conventional economic
wisdom needs revision based on careful empirical analysis. The minimum
wage debate has been fundamentally transformed by his evidence, with many
countries raising their minimum wages based on his findings.

The methodological contributions of Angrist and Imbens have been recognized
as fundamental advances in econometric theory. Their work has shaped how
economists design empirical studies and interpret evidence. The emphasis
on causal identification has raised the bar for empirical research across
all fields of economics.

Their collaborative work has inspired a new generation of economists to
think carefully about identification and causal mechanisms. The emphasis
on rigorous empirical methods has transformed how economics is practiced.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
The methods developed by Card, Angrist, and Imbens are now standard tools
in empirical economics. Randomized controlled trials, regression
discontinuity designs, and instrumental variables are widely used across
all subfields. The causal inference revolution has transformed how
economists think about evidence and policy evaluation.

Their work has influenced policy debates about minimum wages, immigration,
education, and many other topics. The empirical evidence they generated
has shaped policy discussions worldwide. Their methods have been applied
to analyze the effects of welfare reform, criminal justice policies, and
healthcare interventions.

The methodological framework continues to be refined and extended. Recent
work has applied their methods to new areas like platform economics, climate
policy, and artificial intelligence. The emphasis on credible causal
identification remains central to empirical economic research. Modern
research on algorithmic decision-making draws on their insights about
causal inference.

Their collaborative work has inspired new research programs in causal
inference and empirical economics. The emphasis on rigorous methods has
influenced research standards across the social sciences, with other
fields adopting their approaches to causal identification.
