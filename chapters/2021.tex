\chapter{2021 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }David Card (Canada/USA), Joshua Angrist (USA), Guido Imbens (Netherlands/USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For David Card's empirical contributions to labour economics and Joshua Angrist
and Guido Imbens's methodological contributions to the analysis of causal
relationships

\section{What They Did}
David Card, Joshua Angrist, and Guido Imbens advanced empirical economics through
complementary contributions. Card used natural experiments to study labor-market
questions, while Angrist and Imbens developed methods for interpreting causal
effects from instrumental variables. Together, their work helped establish new
standards for credible empirical research.

David Card studied the effects of minimum-wage increases on employment,
challenging the conventional view that higher minimum wages must reduce
employment. His New Jersey--Pennsylvania study found no significant employment
decline, spurring extensive research on how labor-market institutions and market
power affect wages and employment. He also examined immigration's effects on
wages and employment. His work on the Mariel boatlift found no substantial
short-run effect on the wages of less-skilled workers already living in Miami,
though immigration can affect different groups of workers differently.

Angrist and Imbens made fundamental contributions to the methodology of
causal inference. They developed the concept of local average treatment
effect (LATE), showing how instrumental variables identify causal effects
for specific subpopulations. Their work clarified when and how causal
effects can be identified from observational data, providing rigorous
foundations for the use of instrumental variables in empirical research.
The LATE framework has become a standard tool in econometrics.

Card also studied the effects of education on earnings, using natural
experiments such as changes in compulsory-schooling laws.
His work showed that the returns to education are substantial and that
causal effects can be identified using credible research designs.

Angrist's work on the Vietnam War draft lottery used the draft lottery
number as an instrument to estimate the causal effect of military service
on earnings. His research showed that military service had negative
effects on civilian earnings, challenging conventional wisdom about the
value of military experience.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Their work transformed empirical economics by providing rigorous methods for
identifying causal effects from observational data. Earlier empirical studies
often faced serious identification problems; their methods provided tools for
making the assumptions behind causal interpretation explicit.

The LATE framework has been applied to questions ranging from the effects of
education on earnings to the impact of health insurance on medical outcomes. It
has become an important tool in empirical economics, while its interpretation
depends on assumptions such as instrument relevance, exclusion, and monotonicity.
The emphasis on credible identification has raised standards across empirical
research.

Card's empirical findings on minimum wages, immigration, and education have
influenced policy debates. His work showed that some conventional economic
claims need to be tested against careful empirical evidence, while later
research has continued to debate the size and conditions of these effects.

The methodological contributions of Angrist and Imbens have been recognized
as fundamental advances in econometric theory. Their work has shaped how
economists design empirical studies and interpret evidence. The emphasis
on causal identification has raised the bar for empirical research across
all fields of economics.

Their combined contributions have inspired a new generation of economists to
think carefully about identification and causal mechanisms. The emphasis on
rigorous empirical methods has changed how economics is practiced.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
The methods associated with Card, Angrist, and Imbens are now important tools
in empirical economics. Natural experiments and instrumental variables are
widely used, alongside randomized controlled trials, regression-discontinuity
designs, and other research designs developed by a broader community. The
credibility revolution has changed how economists think about evidence and
policy evaluation.

Their work has influenced policy debates about minimum wages, immigration,
education, and many other topics. Their methods have been applied to analyze
welfare reform, criminal-justice policies, and healthcare interventions, with
results interpreted in light of each study's assumptions and setting.

The methodological framework continues to be refined and extended. Recent work
has applied causal-inference methods to areas such as platform economics,
climate policy, and algorithmic decision-making. The emphasis on credible causal
identification remains central to empirical economic research, although new
contexts often require additional assumptions and designs.

Their combined contributions have inspired new research programs in causal
inference and empirical economics. The emphasis on rigorous methods has
influenced research standards across the social sciences, with other fields
adapting related approaches to causal identification.
