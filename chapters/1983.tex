\chapter{1983 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Gérard Debreu (France/USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his having incorporated new analytical methods into economic theory and for
his rigorous reformulation of the theory of general equilibrium

\section{What They Did}
Gérard Debreu provided the mathematical proof of the existence of competitive
equilibrium using convex set theory, transforming general equilibrium into a
mathematically rigorous discipline. Born in Saint-Cloud, France in 1921,
Debreu studied mathematics at the École Normale Supérieure before moving to
the United States in 1948, where he worked at the Cowles Commission and
later at the University of California, Berkeley.

His seminal 1959 book "Theory of Value" established general equilibrium
theory on proper mathematical foundations. Using fixed-point theorems from
topology, particularly the Kakutani fixed-point theorem, Debreu proved that
under reasonable assumptions about preferences and production sets, a
competitive equilibrium exists. This result resolved long-standing questions
about whether markets could reach equilibrium.

Debreu showed that under convex preferences and continuous functions with
well-behaved production sets, competitive equilibrium exists. His proof
required sophisticated mathematical tools including set-valued analysis and
convex geometry. The mathematical rigor he brought to economics elevated the
standards of economic proof and argument.

He contributed to utility theory, proving existence of continuous utility
functions representing ordinal preferences. This foundational result showed
that consumer preferences could be represented by mathematical functions
under mild conditions.

Debreu provided rigorous treatment of arbitrage-free pricing in complete
markets. He showed that in the absence of arbitrage opportunities, asset
prices can be expressed as expectations of future payoffs discounted at
appropriate rates. This work established the foundation for modern asset
pricing theory and financial economics.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Debreu's work transformed general equilibrium from heuristic reasoning into a
rigorous mathematical discipline, enabling precise statements about existence,
uniqueness, and stability of equilibria. His formulation provided the
mathematical foundation for much of modern microeconomic theory.

By establishing general equilibrium under convexity conditions, he clarified
when markets achieve Pareto optimality. The First and Second Fundamental
Theorems of Welfare Economics, which Debreu helped formalize, showed that
competitive equilibria are Pareto optimal under certain conditions, and
that any Pareto optimal allocation can be achieved as a competitive
equilibrium with appropriate redistribution.

His mathematical approach influenced generations of theorists who extend
his framework to incomplete markets, dynamic settings, and international
trade. The general equilibrium framework became the standard tool for
analyzing the efficiency and stability of economic systems.

Debreu demonstrated that rigorous mathematics can address fundamental
economic questions about market efficiency and welfare, elevating the
standards of economic proof and argument. His work provided the theoretical
foundations for much of modern microeconomics, including consumer theory,
producer theory, and welfare economics.

The mathematical rigor he introduced became the standard for theoretical
economics. His work influenced not only economics but also mathematics,
particularly in the development of fixed-point theorems and convex analysis.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
General equilibrium theory remains a cornerstone of theoretical economics,
with Debreu's formulation providing essential mathematical tools. Existence,
uniqueness, and stability analyses build upon his foundational work.

Asset pricing theory relies on his arbitrage-free characterization of
security values. The fundamental theorem of asset pricing, which links
no-arbitrage to the existence of equivalent martingale measures, extends
Debreu's insights to financial markets.

Extensions to incomplete markets, dynamic general equilibrium, search
frictions, and heterogeneous agents all depend on the mathematical clarity
Debreu established. His framework continues to guide research on market
failures, public goods, and externalities.

Debreu's legacy endures in the mathematical rigor of modern economics. His
approach to rigorous proof and precise analysis remains the standard for
theoretical research. The general equilibrium framework he helped develop
continues to be used in macroeconomic modeling, financial economics, and
international trade theory.
