\chapter{2017 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Richard Thaler (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his contributions to behavioral economics

\section{What They Did}
Richard Thaler advanced behavioral economics by incorporating psychological
insights into economic analysis. Born in East Orange, New Jersey in 1945,
Thaler studied at the University of Rochester and MIT before becoming a
professor at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business.

He identified systematic deviations from rational behavior, including mental
accounting, where people treat money differently depending on its source or
intended use. Thaler showed that people maintain separate mental accounts for
different purposes, leading to inconsistent financial decisions that violate
the principle of fungibility. People budget by category rather than treating
all income as interchangeable.

Thaler documented the endowment effect, showing that people value items more
highly simply because they own them. In experiments, subjects given a mug
demanded significantly more to sell it than they were willing to pay to buy
one, demonstrating that ownership creates value. This finding challenged the
standard economic assumption that value is independent of endowment.

He analyzed present bias, demonstrating how people discount future rewards
more steeply than exponential discounting predicts. Thaler showed that people
often prefer immediate gratification over larger future rewards, leading to
procrastination, inadequate saving, and unhealthy behaviors. This quasi-hyperbolic
discounting model has been widely adopted in economics and public policy.

Thaler's concept of nudging showed how choice architecture can help people
make better decisions without restricting freedom. He argued that since choice
architecture is unavoidable, it should be designed to help people achieve their
own goals. His book "Nudge" (2008), co-authored with Cass Sunstein, popularized
these ideas and influenced policy design worldwide.

His book "Misbehaving" (2015) traced the development of behavioral economics
as a field, documenting the resistance he faced from mainstream economists
who viewed his work as anathema to the rational agent paradigm. Thaler's
advocacy helped establish behavioral economics as a legitimate and important
field of study.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Thaler transformed economics by showing that traditional assumptions about
rationality are too restrictive. His work demonstrated that people have limited
self-control, limited forethought, and limited sympathy for others. These
insights explained puzzles that standard economics could not, such as the
equity premium puzzle, the disposition effect in trading, and the low savings
rates observed across developed countries.

The endowment effect, mental accounting, and present bias have been documented
in thousands of experiments worldwide, establishing behavioral economics as
an empirical reality rather than a theoretical curiosity. These findings have
influenced finance, marketing, public policy, and organizational behavior.

Thaler's nudging concept influenced policy design worldwide, leading to
automatic enrollment in retirement plans, organ donor registration, and
healthier food choices in cafeterias. The UK's Behavioral Insights Team,
established in 2010, became a model for other governments seeking to apply
behavioral insights to policy challenges.

His work bridged economics and psychology, creating a more realistic foundation
for understanding human behavior. The recognition of bounded rationality led to
more humane and effective policy interventions that respect people's actual
decision-making processes rather than assuming perfect rationality.

Thaler's contributions earned him recognition as a pioneer who made economics
more relevant to real-world problems. He demonstrated that economics can be
both scientifically rigorous and practically useful, opening the door for
interdisciplinary research that combines insights from psychology, neuroscience,
and computer science.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Behavioral economics has become mainstream, with Thaler's insights influencing
policy, business, and academic research. Automatic enrollment programs have
dramatically increased retirement savings rates, with participation jumping from
around 49 percent to 86 percent after opt-out systems were adopted.

Nudge units in governments worldwide apply behavioral insights to improve policy
outcomes. Over 60 countries have established behavioral insight teams, and the
World Bank has incorporated behavioral economics into its development programs.
Financial products increasingly incorporate behavioral considerations, such as
default options, simplified disclosures, and automatic escalation of contributions.

The field has expanded to address climate change, public health, and education
policy. Thaler's recent work on eudaimonic economics explores how well-being
and flourishing can guide policy beyond traditional welfare measures. His
concept of "choice architecture" has influenced product design, website
interfaces, and organizational decision-making processes.

As behavioral economics matures, its insights continue to reshape how economists
understand decision-making, design institutions, and evaluate policy interventions.
The 2017 Nobel Prize recognized Thaler's transformative contribution to making
economics more realistic and relevant to the world as it actually is.
