\chapter{1976 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Milton Friedman (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his achievements in the fields of consumption analysis, monetary history and theory, and for
his demonstration of the complexity of stabilization policy

\section{What They Did}
Milton Friedman developed the permanent-income hypothesis, proposing that consumption depends
more heavily on expected long-run or permanent income than on temporary changes in current income.
This hypothesis helped explain why temporary tax changes may have smaller effects on consumption
than lasting changes in household resources.

He provided a historical analysis of money's role in economic fluctuations, challenging prevailing
Keynesian interpretations by emphasizing the quantity theory of money. His monetarist analysis
linked sustained inflation to monetary expansion and favored a more stable monetary framework over
frequent discretionary interventions. His analysis with Anna Schwartz argued that monetary
contraction, together with failures of monetary policy, greatly deepened and prolonged the Great
Depression.

Friedman demonstrated that the Phillips-curve trade-off between inflation and unemployment is only
temporary, as expectations adjust. He developed the theory of the natural rate of unemployment,
arguing that attempts to keep unemployment below this rate would eventually accelerate inflation.
Friedman also contributed to the methodology of controlled and natural experiments and to the
measurement of money demand.

His advocacy for flexible exchange rates influenced currency-regime debates worldwide. He used
adaptive-expectations reasoning to explain how people revise expectations based on past experience.
Friedman's work on the consumption function reshaped macroeconomic analysis by emphasizing
forward-looking assessments of income.

Friedman also analyzed the effects of government spending and taxation in relation to aggregate
demand and monetary policy. His work on the money-demand function provided a theoretical foundation
for monetarist policy recommendations. Friedman's \textit{A Monetary History of the United States,
1867--1960} (1963), co-authored with Anna Schwartz, provided a comprehensive analysis of monetary
events in American history.

Friedman's monetary history emphasized the importance of monetary factors in explaining economic
fluctuations and challenged interpretations that gave fiscal policy the primary role in
stabilization.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Friedman reinvigorated monetary theory, arguing that monetary policy is a major determinant of
nominal economic fluctuations. He was skeptical of frequent discretionary stabilization and
advocated a more predictable monetary framework, including a rule for steady monetary growth.

His monetarist critique contributed to broader debates about regulation, government intervention,
individual freedom, and market solutions. The permanent-income hypothesis reshaped
consumption-saving analysis by linking macroeconomic behavior to household decisions about lifetime
resources.

Friedman's work on the natural rate of unemployment clarified the limits of monetary policy,
showing why attempts to maintain unemployment below its sustainable level could accelerate
inflation. His analysis of the Great Depression emphasized the importance of central-bank actions
and monetary contraction in explaining the severity of the downturn.

Friedman's policy recommendations influenced later debates about rules-based monetary policy and
inflation targeting. His critique of discretionary stabilization emphasized policy lags, the
uncertainty surrounding interventions, and the importance of credible commitments. The
expectations-augmented Phillips curve became central to macroeconomic theory, incorporating his
insights about inflation expectations.

His work on the permanent-income hypothesis influenced consumer theory and the analysis of saving
behavior. Friedman's advocacy for school vouchers and market-oriented public services influenced
debates about education policy and government provision. His ideas about expectations in economic
decision-making have had lasting influence on macroeconomic theory.

Friedman's influence extended beyond academic economics to public policy debates, where his
arguments for free markets and limited government intervention shaped political discourse in
many countries.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Monetarist ideas continue to influence debates in central banking, including discussions of
inflation targeting and the limits of discretionary policy. His arguments against fine-tuning also
resonate in debates about quantitative easing and other unconventional monetary tools.

The permanent-income hypothesis remains an important part of modern consumption theory, informing
models of saving, borrowing, and wealth accumulation. Friedman influenced supply-side economics
and policy debates in the Reagan--Thatcher era through his emphasis on incentives and market
mechanisms. His advocacy for flexible exchange rates influenced debates about currency regimes.

School-choice and privatization debates draw on his arguments for market-oriented provision. The
ongoing tension between rules and discretion in monetary policy reflects Friedman's insights.
His legacy persists in how economists think about the limits of government intervention, the
importance of expectations, and the fundamental role of monetary stability in economic performance.

The 2008 financial crisis prompted renewed discussion of monetary policy, banking stability, and
the lender-of-last-resort function. Modern central banking incorporates some of Friedman's
insights about credibility, monetary conditions, and policy rules, while also drawing on approaches
that differ from monetarism. His work on U.S. monetary history remains a classic reference for
understanding the role of monetary policy in economic fluctuations.

Contemporary debates about monetary policy rules versus discretion, the role of central banks,
and the importance of credible commitments continue to engage with Friedman's insights. His
work on the expectations-augmented Phillips curve remains central to modern macroeconomic theory
and policy analysis.
