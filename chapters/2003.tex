\chapter{2003 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Robert Engle (USA), Clive Granger (UK/Canada)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For methods of analyzing economic time series with time-varying volatility
(ARCH), and for methods of analyzing economic time series with common trends
(cointegration)

\section{What They Did}
Engle and Granger developed complementary econometric techniques for analyzing
time series data that have become indispensable tools in macroeconomics,
finance, and econometrics more broadly. Their work addressed fundamental
problems in time series analysis that had puzzled economists for decades.

Engle developed the Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (ARCH) model
and its generalization, the Generalized ARCH (GARCH) model, to address the
empirical observation that financial time series exhibit volatility clustering.
Asset returns often display periods of high variability followed by further high
variability, and periods of calm followed by further calm. Basic regression
models commonly assume constant variance (homoskedasticity), an assumption that
can be inadequate for financial data. ARCH models allow the conditional variance
to depend on past squared innovations (residuals), capturing this clustering
pattern.

GARCH models, introduced by Tim Bollerslev, add persistence by allowing past
conditional variances to enter as well, producing more parsimonious
representations of volatility dynamics. The GARCH(1,1) model--with one lag of
the squared innovation and one lag of the conditional variance--has become the
workhorse of financial econometrics. These models became important tools in risk
management and financial econometrics, and they can also be used as inputs to
some option-pricing models.

Granger developed the concept of cointegration, which helps address spurious
regression. When regressing one non-stationary time series on another, standard
tests can indicate a significant relationship even when none exists, because both
series may trend over time. Granger and Engle showed that non-stationary series,
often integrated of the same order, can have a linear combination that is
stationary, indicating a long-run equilibrium relationship. Such series are said
to be cointegrated; sharing an integration order alone is not sufficient.

The error-correction mechanism captures how variables may adjust toward their
long-run equilibrium after a shock. If a variable deviates from its
cointegrating relationship, an error-correction term can capture systematic
adjustment toward equilibrium. This framework reconciles short-run dynamics with
long-run equilibrium relationships, subject to the model's specification.

Granger also developed the concept commonly called Granger causality, a notion
of predictive precedence rather than structural causation. If past values of X
improve forecasts of Y after conditioning on the model's other variables, X is
said to Granger-cause Y. Omitted variables, contemporaneous feedback, structural
breaks, and model misspecification can make this result a poor guide to causal
effects.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Engle's ARCH family of models became the foundation of financial econometrics.
Volatility clustering is now recognized as a fundamental feature of financial
returns, and GARCH-type models are standard tools for volatility forecasting.
Value-at-Risk calculations used by banks and regulators can incorporate
GARCH-type volatility forecasts, alongside historical simulation, stress tests,
and other risk models. The ARCH contribution helped change how economists and
financial analysts model time-varying risk.

Granger's cointegration methodology transformed macroeconomic analysis.
Long-run equilibrium relationships--between money and prices, between exchange
rates and purchasing power parity, between consumption and permanent income--can
now be estimated rigorously. Error correction models capture how variables
adjust toward equilibrium, providing insights into the dynamics of economic
adjustment and the speed of mean reversion.

The Granger-causality test, while often misunderstood and misapplied, remains
widely used to examine predictive relationships between variables. VAR models
predate cointegration, while vector error-correction models incorporate
cointegrating restrictions; these tools are used for macroeconomic forecasting,
impulse-response analysis, and policy research.

Their combined work made important contributions to modern time-series
econometrics. ARCH/GARCH models, cointegration tests, and error-correction models
became standard tools for empirical researchers. The Engle--Granger two-step
procedure is useful under its assumptions, while alternatives such as Johansen's
system methods are often preferred for multivariate systems.

The methods they developed have been applied to a wide range of economic
questions, from testing purchasing power parity to analyzing the relationship
between money and prices. Their work provided tools for distinguishing
spurious regression from genuine economic relationships.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Financial econometrics has expanded Engle's innovations to include asymmetric
volatility models such as EGARCH and GJR-GARCH that capture the leverage
effect. Multivariate models such as DCC-GARCH allow dynamic correlations
between assets, improving portfolio diversification and risk management.

Cointegration techniques have been extended to panel data, allowing
cross-sectional information to be analyzed alongside temporal dynamics. Vector
error-correction models remain useful for macroeconomic analysis. Structural VAR
identification is a related but distinct framework that requires additional
economic restrictions; it should not be inferred from a Granger-causality test
alone.

Machine-learning approaches are being combined with classical econometric
methods, though the Engle--Granger framework remains foundational. Both
laureates' work continues to underpin empirical research, risk management,
and policy analysis across economics and finance.

Their innovations have been applied to diverse areas including commodity
prices, exchange rates, interest rates, and asset returns. The concepts
of volatility clustering and cointegration are now standard tools in
empirical economics. Modern financial risk management can use GARCH models for
volatility forecasting and risk measurement, alongside many other models and
stress-testing methods.

The Engle-Granger methodology has influenced how economists think about
long-run relationships in economic data. Their work provided the tools
for distinguishing between spurious correlations and genuine economic
relationships, a fundamental issue in time series analysis.
