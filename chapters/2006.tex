\chapter{2006 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Edmund Phelps (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his analysis of intertemporal tradeoffs in macroeconomic policy

\section{What They Did}
Edmund Phelps made two major contributions to macroeconomic theory that
reshaped our understanding of inflation, unemployment, and the limits of
stabilization policy.

Phelps developed the natural-rate-of-unemployment hypothesis, independently
and contemporaneously with Milton Friedman, demonstrating that the Phillips
curve tradeoff between inflation and unemployment is not permanent. In his
seminal 1967 paper, ``Phillips Curves, Expectations of Inflation and Optimal
Unemployment over Time,'' Phelps argued that attempts to push unemployment below
its equilibrium rate through expansionary policy would produce only temporary
gains if workers and firms adjusted their inflation expectations upward.

The equilibrium or natural rate depends on structural features of the economy,
including labor-market institutions, worker and firm characteristics, matching
processes, and the policy environment. It is not a fixed constant and can shift
over time as these underlying conditions change.

Phelps developed the expectations-augmented Phillips curve, which incorporates
inflation expectations into the relationship between inflation and unemployment.
In a simple representation, the equation is
$\pi_t = \pi_t^e - \alpha (u_t-u_t^*) + \nu_t$,
where $\pi_t$ is inflation, $\pi_t^e$ expected inflation, $u_t$ unemployment,
$u_t^*$ the natural or equilibrium rate, and $\nu_t$ a supply shock. This
formulation showed that the tradeoff is not permanent once expected inflation
adjusts, leaving only a short-run effect of unexpected inflation on unemployment.

Phelps also developed the analysis of intertemporal macroeconomic policy,
examining how current decisions affect future welfare. His work on saving,
capital formation, and the distribution of consumption across generations showed
how foregoing consumption today can raise future productive capacity, while also
creating distributional tradeoffs. He made important contributions to growth
theory, including the role of human capital and innovation in the diffusion of
technology.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Phelps' natural-rate hypothesis transformed macroeconomic understanding of the
inflation-unemployment relationship. It showed why monetary policy cannot
permanently reduce unemployment below an equilibrium rate simply by generating
more inflation; demand stimulus can affect employment in the short run, while
the long-run outcome depends on expectations and labor-market conditions. This
challenged the belief that policymakers could maintain a stable, permanent
tradeoff between higher inflation and lower unemployment.

The distinction between cyclical unemployment (caused by insufficient demand)
and structural unemployment (caused by mismatches between worker skills and
job requirements, or by institutional rigidities) became crucial for policy
formulation. Policies aimed at reducing cyclical unemployment through demand
stimulus may be appropriate during recessions, while structural unemployment
calls for measures such as education, training, improved matching, and
institutional reform. The appropriate policy mix depends on the source of
unemployment and on its likely effects on wages, prices, and participation.

The natural-rate concept became a central part of macroeconomic analysis and the
expectations-augmented Phillips curve became standard in economics textbooks.
The absence of a permanent inflation-unemployment tradeoff contributed to the
emphasis many central banks place on price stability, although monetary-policy
mandates and priorities differ across institutions.

Phelps' work on intertemporal optimization influenced dynamic macroeconomic
models and research on saving, capital formation, and intergenerational welfare.
His analysis of expectations and the future effects of current policy also
complements later work on policy credibility and commitment, including Kydland
and Prescott's contribution.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
The natural-rate hypothesis continues to inform central-bank analysis and
communication. Monetary-policy frameworks seek to anchor expectations about
future policy paths, which can affect the cost of disinflation. The natural rate
and the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU) are related but
not identical concepts, and both are difficult to estimate in real time. Central
banks therefore use such estimates alongside broader indicators when assessing
monetary conditions.

The natural rate is not static. Contemporary research explores how structural
factors--demographic changes, technological disruption, globalization, the
rise of the gig economy, occupational licensing reforms, and changes in labor
market institutions--shift the natural rate over time. Understanding these
dynamics is essential for calibrating monetary policy and designing effective
structural reforms.

Fiscal-policy analysis examines the sustainability implications of borrowing and
spending, recognizing that current deficits can constrain future policy options.
Intertemporal budget constraints and Phelps's analysis of saving and capital
formation remind policymakers that today's choices affect future resources and
the distribution of welfare across generations.

Phelps's broader contributions to growth theory continue to influence research
on innovation, human capital, and economic development. His insights about
expectations and intertemporal tradeoffs remain part of modern monetary theory
and policy analysis, alongside later models and empirical evidence.
