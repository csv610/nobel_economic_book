\chapter{1978 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Herbert Simon (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his pioneering research into the decision-making process within economic
organizations

\section{What They Did}
Herbert Simon introduced the concept of bounded rationality, arguing that human
decision-making is limited by cognitive capacity and information-processing
constraints. Born in Wisconsin in 1916, Simon studied political science and
developed a broad base in economics, mathematics, and psychology. He joined the
faculty of Carnegie Institute of Technology in 1949, later Carnegie Mellon
University, where he conducted most of his academic career. He also received
the A.M. Turing Award in computer science, making him one of the few people to
receive major recognition in both economics and computing.

Simon's concept of bounded rationality challenged the neoclassical assumption
of perfect rationality. He argued that humans are "intendedly rational, but
only marginally so." People face cognitive limitations, incomplete information,
and time constraints that prevent them from maximizing utility in the way
classical economics assumes. This insight provided a more realistic foundation
for understanding human behavior.

Instead of fully optimizing, people ``satisfice''--seeking solutions that are good
enough rather than optimal. Simon showed that search for optimal solutions
is often impractical because the number of possible alternatives and outcomes
is too large to evaluate comprehensively. Satisficing behavior is rational
given cognitive constraints and the cost of information gathering.

He developed the concept of administrative behavior, explaining how
organizations actually make decisions under uncertainty and information
limitations. Organizations develop routines, standard operating procedures,
and heuristics to cope with complexity. These organizational mechanisms
allow decision-makers to function effectively despite bounded rationality.

Simon also made foundational contributions to artificial intelligence.
Working with Allen Newell and others, he helped develop the Logic Theorist and
General Problem Solver, early programs that demonstrated machine
problem-solving capabilities. His work on
cognitive psychology explored how humans process information and solve
problems, influencing the development of cognitive science.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Simon challenged the assumption of unbounded rationality in economics by
showing how decision-makers operate under cognitive and informational limits.
This helped establish foundations for behavioral and organizational studies of
decision-making and challenged traditional assumptions about utility
maximization.

His work showed how organizations make decisions under uncertainty,
emphasizing satisficing and the use of routines and heuristics. These insights
revealed how actual decision-making differs from idealized models,
explaining organizational inefficiencies, path dependence, and inertia.

Simon's analysis of administrative behavior provided alternatives to the
optimizing paradigm. He showed that bounded rationality leads to different
predictions about behavior than perfect rationality. Organizations develop
routines and standard procedures that constrain choice and simplify
decision-making.

His work influenced economics, psychology, computer science, and management
theory, creating important interdisciplinary bridges. Satisficing became a
widely used concept for describing decisions made when optimization is costly
or infeasible.

Simon's work on organizational decision-making revealed how routines and
standard procedures can simplify choices and help organizations function under
complexity. His insights about the limits of rationality
broadened economic analysis beyond idealized individual optimization.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Bounded rationality informs behavioral economics and research on heuristics,
biases, and organizational decision-making. Kahneman and Tversky's work on
judgment under uncertainty was influenced by the broader question Simon raised
about cognitive limitations, while developing its own theories and evidence.

Simon's organizational insights inform research on corporate governance,
strategy, and artificial intelligence. Cognitive science and distributed
problem-solving also draw on his work about how organizations process
information.

Decision-theoretic models incorporate cognitive limits and heuristic
strategies, moving beyond rational expectation assumptions. Behavioral
organizational studies examine how groups make decisions under uncertainty,
building on Simon's analysis of administrative behavior.

His work on problem representation and search influenced computer science and
AI development. Later computational methods have extended these ideas, although
modern machine-learning systems do not simply reproduce Simon's models of human
cognition.

Simon's interdisciplinary approach, combining psychology, sociology, and
economics to study organizations realistically, continues to inspire research.
The recognition that humans face limits on attention, information, and
computation remains an important complement to models of idealized optimization.
