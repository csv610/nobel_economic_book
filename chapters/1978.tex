\chapter{1978 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Herbert Simon (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his pioneering research into the decision-making process within economic organizations

\section{What They Did}
Herbert Simon introduced the concept of bounded rationality, arguing that human decision-making is limited by cognitive capacity and information processing constraints. He showed that rather than optimizing, people satisfice—seeking satisfactory rather than optimal solutions. Simon argued that organizational decision processes involve sequential search and gradual improvement rather than comprehensive optimization. His work on organizational theory emphasized administrative behavior and procedural rationality instead of perfect optimization. He demonstrated that organizations develop routines, standard operating procedures, and heuristics to cope with complexity. Simon also made foundational contributions to artificial intelligence, cognitive psychology, and decision theory. His concept of administrative behavior explained how organizations actually make decisions under uncertainty and information limitations, emphasizing incrementalism and satisficing heuristics.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Simon fundamentally altered the understanding of rationality in economics, replacing the perfect rationality assumption with bounded rationality. This created the foundation for behavioral and organizational studies of decision-making, challenging traditional neoclassical assumptions about utility maximization. His work showed how organizations make decisions under uncertainty and information limitations, emphasizing incrementalism and satisficing heuristics. These insights revealed how actual decision-making differs from idealized models, explaining organizational inefficiencies, path dependence, and inertia. Simon's analysis of administrative behavior provided alternatives to the optimizing paradigm, showing that bounded rationality leads to different predictions about behavior. His work influenced economics, psychology, computer science, and management theory, creating interdisciplinary bridges that enriched all these fields. The concept of satisficing became a standard alternative to optimization in modeling human behavior.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Bounded rationality underlies behavioral economics, prospect theory, mental accounting, and research on heuristics and biases. Simon's organizational insights inform corporate governance, strategy, and artificial intelligence. Cognitive science and distributed problem-solving draw on his work about how organizations process information. Decision-theoretic models incorporate cognitive limits and heuristic strategies, moving beyond rational expectation assumptions. Behavioral organizational studies examine how groups make decisions under uncertainty, building on Simon's analysis of administrative behavior. His work on knowledge representation influenced computer science and AI development. Simon's interdisciplinary approach combining psychology, sociology, and economics studying organizations in realistic ways continues to inspire research. The recognition that humans have limited rationality has transformed economics from a study of perfect optimizers to analysis of real decision-makers facing constraints.
