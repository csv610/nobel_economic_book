\chapter{1978 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Herbert Simon (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his pioneering research into the decision-making process within economic organizations

\section{What They Did}
Herbert Simon introduced the concept of bounded rationality, arguing that human decision-making
is limited by cognitive capacity and information processing constraints. He showed that rather
than optimizing, people satisfice—seeking satisfactory rather than optimal solutions.

Simon argued that organizational decision processes involve sequential search and gradual
improvement rather than comprehensive optimization. His work on organizational theory emphasized
administrative behavior and procedural rationality instead of perfect optimization. He
demonstrated that organizations develop routines, standard operating procedures, and heuristics to
cope with complexity.

Simon also made foundational contributions to artificial intelligence, cognitive psychology, and
decision theory. He developed the concept of administrative behavior, explaining how organizations
actually make decisions under uncertainty and information limitations. Simon conducted pioneering
research on problem-solving and human cognition, developing computational models of human
thinking. His work on decision-making under uncertainty examined how people make choices when
facing incomplete information.

Simon developed the concept of satisficing as an alternative to optimization, arguing that search
for optimal solutions is often impractical. His research on organizational learning examined how
organizations adapt and change over time. Simon's interdisciplinary work bridged economics,
psychology, computer science, and political science.

Simon's work on artificial intelligence included the development of the Logic Theorist and the
General Problem Solver, early programs that demonstrated machine problem-solving capabilities.
His research on human cognition led to the development of information processing models of
thinking and decision-making. Simon's book "Administrative Behavior" (1947) provided a detailed
analysis of organizational decision-making processes.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Simon fundamentally altered the understanding of rationality in economics, replacing the perfect
rationality assumption with bounded rationality. This created the foundation for behavioral and
organizational studies of decision-making, challenging traditional neoclassical assumptions about
utility maximization.

His work showed how organizations make decisions under uncertainty and information limitations,
emphasizing incrementalism and satisficing heuristics. These insights revealed how actual
decision-making differs from idealized models, explaining organizational inefficiencies, path
dependence, and inertia. Simon's analysis of administrative behavior provided alternatives to the
optimizing paradigm, showing that bounded rationality leads to different predictions about behavior.

His work influenced economics, psychology, computer science, and management theory, creating
interdisciplinary bridges that enriched all these fields. The concept of satisficing became a
standard alternative to optimization in modeling human behavior. Simon's work on organizational
decision-making revealed how routines and standard procedures serve as cognitive shortcuts that
enable organizations to function effectively. His insights about the limits of rationality
challenged the methodological individualism that dominated economic theory.

Simon's concept of procedural rationality provided a more realistic model of decision-making that
incorporated psychological and organizational constraints. His work on organizational learning
showed how organizations adapt their decision-making procedures over time. Simon's contributions
to artificial intelligence influenced the development of expert systems and machine learning
algorithms.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Bounded rationality underlies behavioral economics, prospect theory, mental accounting, and
research on heuristics and biases. Simon's organizational insights inform corporate governance,
strategy, and artificial intelligence. Cognitive science and distributed problem-solving draw on
his work about how organizations process information.

Decision-theoretic models incorporate cognitive limits and heuristic strategies, moving beyond
rational expectation assumptions. Behavioral organizational studies examine how groups make
decisions under uncertainty, building on Simon's analysis of administrative behavior. His work on
knowledge representation influenced computer science and AI development.

Simon's interdisciplinary approach combining psychology, sociology, and economics studying
organizations in realistic ways continues to inspire research. The recognition that humans have
limited rationality has transformed economics from a study of perfect optimizers to analysis of
real decision-makers facing constraints. Modern behavioral economics, which has won its own Nobel
prizes, builds directly on Simon's insights about bounded rationality.

His work on organizational learning and adaptation has influenced evolutionary economics and the
study of economic change. The practical applications of bounded rationality extend to management
decision-making, public policy design, and the study of organizational behavior. Simon's legacy
endures in the growing recognition that economic models must account for the cognitive and
organizational realities of actual decision-makers. Modern research on bounded rationality
continues to build on Simon's foundational insights about the limits of human cognition.
