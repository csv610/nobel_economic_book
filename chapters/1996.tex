\chapter{1996 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }James Mirrlees (UK), William Vickrey (Canada/USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
Foundational work on economic theory of incentives under asymmetric information

\section{What They Did}
James Mirrlees solved the optimal taxation problem, addressing the tradeoff
between redistribution and efficiency when the government cannot observe
individual ability. Mirrlees demonstrated that taxation must balance revenue
needs against equity goals while minimizing incentive distortions. His
mathematical framework showed how optimal income tax schedules should be designed
to balance efficiency and equity considerations under asymmetric information.

William Vickrey pioneered auction theory, analyzing bidding strategies in
sealed-bid auctions. He revealed the revenue equivalence principle, showing that
different auction formats generate equivalent revenue under certain conditions.
Vickrey studied mechanism design under asymmetric information, where the principal
cannot observe the agent's actions or types. Vickrey introduced the concept of
truthful revelation mechanisms, encouraging participants to reveal their true
valuations.

Vickrey's second-price auction design ensures that bidding one's true value is
a dominant strategy. In a second-price sealed-bid auction, the highest bidder
wins but pays the second-highest bid. This incentive compatibility property
makes truthful bidding optimal regardless of what others do. Vickrey's analysis
laid the groundwork for modern auction theory and mechanism design.

The Vickrey-Mirrlees approach focused on incentive compatibility, ensuring that
participants truthfully reveal private information is necessary for efficient
allocation. Designing contracts and regulatory frameworks that accommodate
informational asymmetries emerged from their complementary works. Their
contributions established fundamental principles governing economic transactions
under information problems.

\subsection*{Optimal Taxation}
Mirrlees showed that when the government cannot observe individual ability,
optimal taxation involves a tradeoff between equity and efficiency. High marginal
tax rates on top earners reduce inequality but discourage effort and
productivity. The optimal tax schedule depends on the distribution of ability
and the elasticity of labor supply. Mirrlees's framework provided the first
rigorous treatment of this classic problem.

\subsection*{Auction Theory}
Vickrey analyzed how different auction formats affect bidding behavior and
revenue. He showed that in independent private value settings, first-price and
second-price auctions generate equivalent expected revenue. This revenue
equivalence principle provides a foundation for auction design. Vickrey's insights
exploded when applied to real-world auctions, from art sales to spectrum
licensing.

\subsection*{Mechanism Design}
Vickrey and Mirrlees established the foundations of mechanism design, the study
of how to design institutions that achieve desired outcomes when agents have
private information. Incentive compatibility requires that agents find it optimal
to reveal their private information. Participation constraints ensure that agents
voluntarily participate in the mechanism. These constraints define the feasible
set of mechanisms.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Mirrlees and Vickrey fundamentally transformed the understanding of incentive
problems arising from asymmetric information. Optimal taxation theory demonstrates
the tradeoff between redistribution and efficiency losses caused by taxation.
Higher top marginal rates reduce inequality but discourage effort provision.

Vickrey's auction analysis revealed efficient allocation mechanisms for private
valuations. The second-price auction's dominant strategy property ensures that
bidding equals true valuation. Revenue equivalence connects different auction
formats, informing auction design choices. The Vickrey-Mirrlees framework informed
regulatory contracting, insurance design, executive compensation, procurement, and
spectrum licensing.

Mechanism design emerged as a major field building on their insights.
Principal-agent problems are addressed through agency contracts that align
interests between parties. Vickrey's truthful revelation inspired the
Myerson-Satterthwaite theorem, which shows that efficient trade in bilateral
trading with asymmetric information is generally impossible.

Mirrlees and Vickrey established that informational asymmetries require explicit
attention in designing institutions. Their work provided a systematic framework
for analyzing incentive compatibility, participation constraints, and
efficiency-loss tradeoffs. These are fundamental constraints for designing
economic organizations and institutions facing information limitations.

\subsection*{Principal-Agent Theory}
The principal-agent framework emerged from Vickrey-Mirrlees insights, analyzing
how contracts should be designed when one party (the agent) has private information
or takes unobservable actions. Moral hazard and adverse selection problems require
careful contract design to align incentives. Principal-agent theory has applications
in corporate governance, insurance, regulation, and public policy.

\subsection*{Regulatory Economics}
Mirrlees's optimal taxation framework influenced regulatory economics, where
regulators must design rules for firms with private information about costs and
demand. Laffont and Tirole extended Mirrlees's approach to regulatory settings,
showing how to balance efficiency and rent extraction. Regulatory contracts
incorporate incentive compatibility constraints to elicit truthful cost reports.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Mechanism design continues expanding on Vickrey-Mirrlees foundations. Auction
formats adapt to combinatorial complexities in multiple-item spectrum auctions,
energy markets, and electricity wholesale markets. Kidney matching and school
assignment algorithms implement Vickrey-inspired truthful revelation mechanisms.

Optimal taxation literature extends Mirrlees's framework, incorporating behavioral
considerations and administrative costs. Progressive tax design debates mirror
Mirrlees's efficiency-equity tradeoffs. Executive compensation contracts use
agency theory reflecting Vickrey-Mirrlees insights about aligning incentives.

Insurance designs accommodate adverse selection by offering screening menus.
Procurement mechanisms incentivize truthful cost reporting. Market design
applications of Vickrey-Mirrlees insights proliferate across domains. Vickrey's
truthful revelation inspired mechanism designers to create systems that elicit
private information efficiently.

Mirrlees's optimal taxation framework informs progressive tax policy discussions.
Vickrey-Mirrlees principles guide designing institutions that cope with asymmetric
information. Their insights remain vital for modern institutional design, from
digital platform regulation to climate policy mechanisms. The Vickrey-Mirrlees
framework continues to inspire research exploring mechanisms that achieve
efficiency under information constraints.
