\chapter{1996 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }James Mirrlees (UK), William Vickrey (Canada/USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their fundamental contributions to the economic theory of incentives under
asymmetric information

\section{What They Did}
James Mirrlees developed a rigorous framework for the optimal-income-tax
problem, addressing the tradeoff between redistribution and efficiency when the
government cannot observe individual ability or effort. His analysis showed how
tax schedules must account for incentive constraints and the distribution of
ability. The resulting method became a general framework for studying policy
under asymmetric information, rather than a single universally optimal tax
schedule.

William Vickrey pioneered modern auction theory, analyzing bidding strategies
and the efficiency of different auction formats. His work contributed to the
conditions behind revenue comparisons across auctions and to the broader theory
of preference revelation under asymmetric information. Vickrey's mechanisms
showed how rules can give participants incentives to reveal private valuations;
his work on auctions is distinct from the later general theory of mechanism
design.

Vickrey's second-price auction gives truthful bidding as a dominant strategy
under the standard single-item, independent-private-values model with
quasi-linear preferences. The highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest
bid. This result made the auction a canonical example of incentive-compatible
design and contributed to modern auction theory.

Their complementary work showed how incentive constraints affect taxation,
auctions, contracts, and other institutions when information is asymmetric.
Truthful revelation can support efficient allocation in some settings, but it
is not always possible or costless. Their contributions established important
principles for analyzing economic transactions under information problems.

\subsection*{Optimal Taxation}
Mirrlees showed that when the government cannot observe individual ability,
optimal taxation involves a tradeoff between redistribution and incentives.
The shape of the tax schedule depends on the social-welfare objective, the
distribution of ability, labor-supply responses, and administrative constraints.
Mirrlees's framework provided a rigorous modern treatment of this problem,
without implying that higher marginal rates always have the same effects.

\subsection*{Auction Theory}
Vickrey analyzed how auction formats affect bidding behavior, allocation, and
revenue. Under specific assumptions, including independent private values and
symmetry, standard auction formats can satisfy revenue-equivalence results;
these results were developed and generalized in later research. Vickrey's
insights contributed to auction design for settings ranging from private sales
to public procurement and spectrum licensing.

\subsection*{Mechanism Design}
Vickrey and Mirrlees made foundational contributions to the later field of
mechanism design, which studies how to design institutions when agents have
private information. Incentive compatibility requires that a participant's
truthful report or prescribed action be optimal under the mechanism, while
participation constraints ensure voluntary participation. These constraints
help define the feasible set of mechanisms.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Mirrlees and Vickrey transformed the analysis of incentive problems arising
from asymmetric information. Optimal-tax theory makes the tradeoff between
redistribution and incentive effects explicit, with conclusions depending on
preferences, technology, ability distributions, and institutional constraints.

Vickrey's auction analysis showed how some rules can achieve efficient
allocation when valuations are private and assumptions are satisfied. In a
standard second-price auction, truthful bidding is dominant. Revenue-equivalence
results connect some auction formats under restrictive conditions. Their work
also informed research on regulatory contracting, insurance, procurement, and
other incentive problems.

Mechanism design emerged as a major field building on their insights and those
of other economists. Principal--agent models study contracts that align
interests when actions or information are hidden. Later work, including the
Myerson--Satterthwaite theorem, shows that efficient bilateral trade is
generally impossible when private information and voluntary participation are
combined with the relevant feasibility assumptions.

Their work emphasized that informational asymmetries must be considered when
designing institutions. The framework analyzes incentive compatibility,
participation constraints, and efficiency tradeoffs in organizations facing
information limitations.

\subsection*{Principal-Agent Theory}
The principal-agent framework draws on related contributions from incentive
theory, analyzing contracts when one party has private information or takes
unobservable actions. Moral hazard and adverse selection require careful contract
design to align incentives. Principal-agent theory has applications in corporate
governance, insurance, regulation, and public policy.

\subsection*{Regulatory Economics}
Mirrlees's optimal-tax framework influenced regulatory economics, where regulators
design rules for firms with private information about costs and demand. Later work
by Jean-Jacques Laffont, Jean Tirole, and others extended incentive methods to
regulatory settings, balancing efficiency and rent extraction. Regulatory
contracts use incentive constraints to encourage accurate cost reporting, although
the appropriate design depends on the institutional setting.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Mechanism design continues to build on Vickrey's auction work, Mirrlees's
incentive methods, and contributions from many other theorists. Auction formats
address combinatorial allocation in spectrum, energy, and procurement markets.
Matching markets such as kidney exchange and school assignment draw primarily on
matching theory and related market-design research, not directly on Vickrey auctions.

Optimal taxation literature extends Mirrlees's framework, incorporating behavioral
considerations and administrative costs. Progressive tax design debates mirror
Mirrlees's efficiency-equity tradeoffs. Executive compensation contracts use
agency theory reflecting Vickrey-Mirrlees insights about aligning incentives.

Insurance designs accommodate adverse selection by offering screening menus.
Procurement mechanisms can use incentive constraints to encourage accurate cost
reports. Market-design applications of these ideas extend across many domains,
while the feasibility of truthful information revelation depends on the mechanism
and its assumptions.

Mirrlees's optimal-tax framework informs progressive tax-policy discussions.
Their methods help economists analyze institutions that cope with asymmetric
information, including some questions in digital-platform regulation and climate
policy. Current research continues to study when mechanisms can achieve desirable
allocations under information constraints.
