\chapter{2011 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Thomas Sargent (USA), Christopher Sims (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their empirical research on cause and effect in the macroeconomy

\section{What They Did}
Thomas Sargent and Christopher Sims developed methods for analyzing cause and
effect in the macroeconomy, particularly how economic policy changes and expectations
interact with macroeconomic variables. Sargent was born in Pasadena, California in
1943, earned his doctorate at Harvard, and spent his career at institutions like
the University of Chicago, Stanford, and NYU. Sims was born in Washington, D.C., in
1942, earned his doctorate at Harvard, and has been a professor at Princeton.

Sargent was a pioneer of the rational-expectations revolution. He showed how
structural macroeconometrics can be used to analyze permanent changes in economic
policy. He demonstrated that households and businesses adjust their expectations
based on their understanding of government policy rules. Therefore, when policy
regimes change (for example, a shift to a new inflation-targeting framework), the
behavior of economic agents can also change, making historical correlations
unreliable for predicting future outcomes. His work helped turn the Lucas critique
into an empirical research agenda. Sargent developed methods to estimate
structural models with rational expectations, providing tools to distinguish
between temporary policy shocks and permanent regime changes. He also analyzed
the fiscal foundations of inflation, co-authoring the influential paper ``Some
Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic,'' which showed how long-run fiscal policy can
constrain monetary policy.

Christopher Sims developed vector autoregression (VAR) methodology for analyzing
cause and effect in macroeconomics. VAR models treat the included variables as
jointly endogenous, allowing researchers to study their dynamic relationships
without imposing a fully specified structural model. To identify structural
shocks, however, researchers must still impose identifying assumptions. Sims'
impulse-response analysis showed how economic variables respond over time to
unexpected changes in monetary policy, fiscal policy, or other shocks. This
approach gave economists a flexible empirical framework for studying policy
effects and macroeconomic dynamics.

Together, their work established modern macroeconometrics and the study of
business cycle dynamics through empirical identification of causal relationships.
Sargent provided the structural macroeconometric methods for policy regime changes,
while Sims provided the vector autoregression tools for tracing the dynamic
effects of policy shocks.

Sargent's work on structural estimation showed that econometric relationships can
change when policy regimes change, because agents adjust their expectations. This
challenged policy models whose estimated relationships were treated as invariant
when policy rules changed.

Sims' VAR methodology provided a flexible alternative to fully specified
structural models. His work showed how to use VAR estimates to study structural
shocks while making the identifying assumptions explicit rather than hiding them
inside a large theoretical system.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Sargent and Sims transformed macroeconomic theory and empirical practice. Sargent's
rational-expectations research challenged approaches that treated expectations as
largely passive, showing that policy effectiveness depends in part on how
expectations are formed. This work contributed to new classical macroeconomics and
recursive macroeconomic theory.

Central banks incorporated Sargent's and Sims' insights into their policy frameworks,
recognizing that credibility and expectations management matter. The idea that
policy effects depend on how agents expect policy to evolve became central to
modern monetary policy design.

Sims' VAR methodology provided empirical tools for testing macroeconomic theories
and analyzing policy effects. Impulse response functions became standard for
communicating how economies respond to shocks. Their work established the
importance of identifying structural shocks rather than merely correlating
variables.

This methodological advance improved empirical macroeconomics across many
subfields. The Lucas critique (which Sargent helped operationalize) remains a
fundamental principle in policy analysis, reminding economists that model parameters
depend on policy regimes. Sims' approach to identification became standard in
applied macroeconomics.

The rational-expectations revolution changed how economists think about policy.
Under particular assumptions, it implies that predictable policy has limited
effects on real variables, shifting attention toward expectations, credibility,
and the design of policy rules in macroeconomic management.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Sims and Sargent's contributions remain foundational in macroeconomic research and
policy. VAR models are widely used for forecasting, policy analysis, and structural
interpretation. Rational expectations remain an important benchmark in modern
macroeconomics, alongside models with learning, bounded rationality, and other
expectations mechanisms.

The methodology developed by Sims has been extended to high-frequency
identification, narrative approaches, and nowcasting. Researchers and some central
banks use related techniques to evaluate policy rules and assess the effects of
unconventional monetary policies such as quantitative easing.

The 2008 financial crisis renewed interest in macroeconomic shocks and propagation
mechanisms, areas where both scholars made key contributions. Research on forward
guidance, quantitative easing, and fiscal multipliers builds on their work.

The rational-expectations hypothesis has been extended with bounded rationality,
adaptive learning, and robust control, addressing criticisms of strong assumptions
about information and expectations. Modern macroeconometric practice continues to
rely on the identification strategies and empirical tools pioneered by Sims and
Sargent.

Their work established the standards for empirical macroeconomics that continue
to guide research today. The emphasis on credible identification and the
importance of expectations remains central to macroeconomic analysis.
