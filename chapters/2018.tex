\chapter{2018 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }William Nordhaus (USA), Paul Romer (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For integrating climate change and technological innovation into long-run
macroeconomic analysis

\section{What They Did}
William Nordhaus developed integrated assessment models that combine economic
growth with climate science, showing how climate change affects economic welfare
and how economic policies affect climate outcomes. Born in Hawaii in 1941,
Nordhaus has been a professor at Yale University for most of his career.

His DICE (Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy) model quantified the costs of
climate change and the benefits of carbon pricing, demonstrating that optimal
policy requires putting a price on carbon emissions. Nordhaus showed that
climate change is an economic problem requiring economic solutions, and that
the social cost of carbon can be estimated using integrated assessment models.

Nordhaus calculated that the optimal carbon price should be around $50 per ton
to internalize the external costs of emissions. His work has been influential
in debates about carbon taxation, emissions trading, and climate policy design.
He argued that market-based mechanisms like carbon pricing are more efficient
than command-and-control regulations for reducing emissions.

Paul Romer developed endogenous growth theory, showing that technological progress
is not exogenous but results from intentional investment in research and
development. Born in Philadelphia in 1955, Romer has been a professor at NYU
and Stanford, and served as World Bank chief economist from 2016 to 2018.

His models demonstrated that ideas are non-rivalrous goods that generate increasing
returns, explaining why knowledge accumulation drives long-run growth. Unlike
physical goods, ideas can be used by infinitely many people simultaneously
without being depleted, creating spillover effects that benefit society as a
whole.

Romer showed that patents and intellectual property rights create incentives for
innovation. He analyzed the optimal design of patent systems, balancing the
need to reward innovators against the cost of monopoly pricing. His work has
influenced debates about pharmaceutical patents, software patents, and open
source licensing.

Together, they provided frameworks for understanding how innovation and climate
policy affect economic development and long-run prosperity. Their combined work
showed that economics must address both the drivers of growth and the constraints
imposed by finite resources and environmental limits.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Nordhaus and Romer transformed macroeconomic analysis by incorporating climate
change and innovation as central phenomena. Nordhaus showed that climate change
is not an external issue but a fundamental economic problem requiring pricing
mechanisms that internalize environmental externalities.

His work influenced the Paris Agreement and carbon pricing debates worldwide.
The concept of the social cost of carbon, which Nordhaus pioneered, is now used
by governments to evaluate climate policies and regulate emissions. The US
Government's interagency working group on social cost of greenhouse gases has
produced estimates ranging from $51 to $190 per ton, depending on discount rates
and damage functions.

Romer's endogenous growth theory explained why some countries grow faster than
others and how intellectual property rights affect innovation incentives. This
influenced patent policy, R\&D subsidies, and education policy. His insights
about the non-rivalrous nature of ideas have implications for copyright law,
open access movements, and the economics of digital goods.

Their combined work showed that long-run growth depends on both technological
progress and environmental sustainability. The integration of climate and growth
models created a new research program addressing the most pressing challenges
of the twenty-first century: how to achieve sustainable prosperity in a
carbon-constrained world.

Nordhaus and Romer demonstrated that economics can address global problems that
transcend national boundaries and require coordinated international action.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Nordhaus and Romer's frameworks are essential for addressing contemporary
challenges. Climate economists use integrated assessment models to evaluate
carbon pricing, adaptation strategies, and climate justice. The economics of
climate change has become a major research area with policy implications
worldwide.

Nordhaus's work informs debates about carbon taxes, emissions trading, and
climate litigation. His recent work on geoengineering examines the economics
of solar radiation management as a potential complement to emission reductions.
The IPCC reports incorporate integrated assessment models based on Nordhaus's
methodology to project future climate impacts.

Romer's endogenous growth theory informs debates about innovation policy,
intellectual property, and the knowledge economy. Research on green growth,
sustainable development, and the circular economy builds on their integrated
approach, examining how to decouple economic growth from environmental degradation.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of innovation for public health,
extending Romer's insights about ideas as public goods. Vaccine development
demonstrated how intellectual property incentives and public funding can work
together to address global health challenges.

As economies face the dual challenges of climate change and technological
transformation, Nordhaus and Romer's frameworks provide essential analytical
tools for designing policies that promote sustainable prosperity. Their work
continues to shape debates about the role of markets, government, and
international cooperation in addressing twenty-first century challenges.
