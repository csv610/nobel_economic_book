\chapter{2018 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }William Nordhaus (USA), Paul Romer (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For integrating climate change and technological innovations into long-run
macroeconomic analysis

\section{What They Did}
William Nordhaus developed integrated assessment models that combine economic
growth with climate science, showing how climate change affects economic welfare
and how economic policies affect climate outcomes. Born in Albuquerque, New Mexico,
in 1941, Nordhaus has been a professor at Yale University for most of his career.

His DICE (Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy) model quantified the economic
consequences of climate change and the potential benefits of carbon pricing.
Under the model's assumptions, a carbon price can make decision-makers account
for emissions' external costs. Nordhaus showed that climate change is an economic
problem as well as a physical one, and that integrated assessment models can be
used to estimate a social cost of carbon.

Nordhaus's estimates of the optimal carbon price depend substantially on the
model's discount rate, climate damages, and other assumptions. His work has been influential
in debates about carbon taxation, emissions trading, and climate policy design.
He argued that market-based mechanisms such as carbon pricing can reduce
emissions efficiently, while recognizing that their performance depends on
measurement, enforcement, distributional effects, and the policy context.

Paul Romer developed endogenous growth theory, showing how technological progress
can result from intentional investment in research and development. Born in
Denver, Colorado, in 1955, Romer has been a professor at NYU
and Stanford, and briefly served as the World Bank's chief economist.

His models demonstrated that ideas are non-rival goods that can generate
increasing returns, helping explain why knowledge accumulation drives long-run
growth. Unlike many physical goods, an idea can be used by multiple people without
being depleted, although access may be restricted and use may sometimes involve
congestion or other costs.

Romer's models showed why intellectual-property protection can create incentives
for innovation, while also imposing costs when it restricts access. This tradeoff
has informed analysis of patent policy, software, and open-source licensing.

Together, they provided frameworks for understanding how innovation and climate
policy affect economic development and long-run prosperity. Their combined work
showed that economics must address both the drivers of growth and the constraints
imposed by finite resources and environmental limits.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Nordhaus and Romer transformed macroeconomic analysis by incorporating climate
change and innovation as central phenomena. Nordhaus showed that climate change
is a fundamental economic problem involving externalities, intergenerational
tradeoffs, and policy choices about how to internalize environmental costs.

His work influenced carbon-pricing debates and the wider use of economic analysis
in climate policy. The concept of the social cost of carbon, to which Nordhaus
made important contributions, is used in some policy analyses to evaluate climate
measures. Estimates vary substantially with discount rates, climate damages,
uncertainty, and the geographic and ethical scope of the analysis.

Romer's endogenous growth theory explained why some countries grow faster than
others and how intellectual property rights affect innovation incentives. This
influenced patent policy, R\&D subsidies, and education policy. His insights
about the non-rivalrous nature of ideas have implications for copyright law,
open access movements, and the economics of digital goods.

Their complementary work showed that long-run growth depends both on technological
progress and on the environmental constraints facing the economy. Together, these
ideas helped motivate research on sustainable prosperity in a carbon-constrained
world, even though Nordhaus and Romer developed distinct frameworks rather than a
single joint model.

Nordhaus and Romer demonstrated that economics can address global problems that
transcend national boundaries and require coordinated international action.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Nordhaus and Romer's frameworks remain important for analyzing contemporary
challenges. Climate economists use integrated assessment models to evaluate
carbon pricing, adaptation strategies, uncertainty, and distributional questions.
The economics of climate change has become a major research area with policy
implications worldwide.

Nordhaus's work informs debates about carbon taxes and emissions trading. Related
research also examines the economics of geoengineering and solar-radiation
management as possible complements or alternatives to emissions reductions, with
substantial uncertainty and governance risks. Integrated assessment models are
used in climate assessments, although they reflect contributions from many
research groups and modeling traditions.

Romer's endogenous growth theory informs debates about innovation policy,
intellectual property, and the knowledge economy. Research on green growth,
sustainable development, and the circular economy draws on related ideas about
innovation incentives and environmental constraints.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of innovation for public health.
Vaccine development provided a prominent case for studying how intellectual-
property incentives, public funding, research collaboration, and access policies
can work together to address global health challenges.

As economies face the dual challenges of climate change and technological
transformation, Nordhaus and Romer's frameworks provide essential analytical
tools for designing policies that promote sustainable prosperity. Their work
continues to shape debates about the role of markets, government, and
international cooperation in addressing twenty-first century challenges.
