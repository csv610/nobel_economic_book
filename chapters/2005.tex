\chapter{2005 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Robert Aumann (Israel), Thomas Schelling (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For having enhanced our understanding of conflict and cooperation through
game-theory analysis

\section{What They Did}
Aumann and Schelling applied game theory to analyze conflict and cooperation,
helping establish it as a framework for understanding strategic interaction
across a wide range of social, political, and economic domains.

Aumann, born in Germany in 1930, made foundational contributions to the
mathematical theory of repeated games. His work helped formalize how cooperation
can be sustained in infinitely repeated interactions when players are
sufficiently patient and actions can be observed well enough for credible
responses. The key insight is that the prospect of future rewards or punishment
for current behavior can deter short-term deviation. Thus cooperation can emerge
in situations where a one-shot game has a defection equilibrium, although the
sustainable outcomes depend on information, patience, payoffs, and equilibrium
refinements.

Aumann also developed the concept of common knowledge, formalizing the
conditions under which players can coordinate on equilibria in coordination
games. Common knowledge requires not only that all players know a fact, but
that they know that everyone knows it, and know that everyone knows that
everyone knows it, ad infinitum. This infinite hierarchy of knowledge can be
important for coordinating expectations and selecting among equilibria in
games with multiple equilibria; the degree of knowledge required depends on the
game and the coordination task.

His introduction of correlated equilibrium in 1974 extended equilibrium analysis
by allowing players to coordinate their strategies through signals from a common
source, such as a mediator or public signal. Unlike a Nash equilibrium in
independent mixed strategies, a correlated equilibrium allows recommendations to
be statistically related, provided that each player has an incentive to follow
the recommendation. The concept is useful for analyzing communication,
mediation, and coordination in many strategic settings.

Schelling, born in Oakland, California, in 1921, applied game-theoretic insights to
military strategy, bargaining, and conflict resolution. In his seminal work
"The Strategy of Conflict" (1960), he analyzed how commitment mechanisms
shape bargaining outcomes. He showed that the ability to constrain one's
own future choices--through irreversible investments, public announcements,
or institutional arrangements--can strengthen one's negotiating position
by making threats credible.

Schelling's insights about focal points showed how players coordinate in
the absence of communication. Focal points are salient solutions that
players naturally converge on without explicit coordination. His analysis
of credibility, commitment, and brinkmanship informed understanding of
nuclear deterrence, international negotiations, and labor disputes.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Aumann and Schelling made complementary contributions to the development and
application of game theory. Aumann's formal analysis of repeated interaction and
knowledge, together with Schelling's strategic analysis of commitment and
coordination, demonstrated the relevance of game-theoretic reasoning to politics,
diplomacy, economics, and other social situations.

Repeated-game theory showed how cooperation can be sustained in ongoing
relationships where future consequences and reputation matter. Schelling's
insights about commitment revealed how the ability to bind oneself strategically
can alter bargaining outcomes. These ideas apply to business relationships,
international negotiations, labor negotiations, and institutional design.

The correlated-equilibrium concept enriched strategic analysis, allowing for
coordination through communication and mediation without requiring binding
contracts. Common knowledge formalized one coordination requirement for shared
understanding, with applications ranging from equilibrium selection in
coordination games to analyses of self-fulfilling expectations and financial
coordination problems.

Their work extended beyond economics into political science, international
relations, evolutionary biology, and philosophy. Schelling's work on nuclear
deterrence during the Cold War had implications well beyond academic economics.
His analysis of credible commitment and brinkmanship informed strategic debates
about deterrence and conflict, although translating such models into policy
required political and military judgments beyond the models themselves.

Aumann's work on epistemic game theory provided rigorous foundations for
analyzing knowledge and belief in strategic situations. His contributions to
repeated games and common knowledge influenced later work on learning,
coordination, and institutions, while evolutionary game theory developed through
additional biological and mathematical traditions.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Repeated-game theory is related to, but distinct from, evolutionary game theory,
where replicator dynamics studies how strategies can change under selection.
Behavioral experiments have examined cooperation in repeated interactions,
including conditions under which cooperation is sustained or breaks down.

Correlated equilibrium is used as an analytical concept in studies of traffic,
spectrum allocation, auctions, and other coordination problems. Mechanism-design
models can incorporate correlated signals to study allocation and incentives.
Matching markets for school assignments, residency placements, and kidney
exchange draw primarily on matching theory, though they also belong to the wider
game-theoretic and market-design literature.

The framework informs industrial organization, labor negotiations, and
corporate governance. Coalition formation, alliances, joint ventures, and
partnerships all employ game-theoretic analysis. The study of international
institutions, treaty compliance, and enforcement mechanisms builds on Aumann
and Schelling's insights about repeated interaction and credible commitment.

Their work has influenced analysis of institutions for managing common-pool
resources, international environmental agreements, and global governance. The
insights about commitment, coordination, and repeated interaction remain relevant
to collective-action problems, although institutional outcomes depend on local
rules, enforcement, information, and political conditions.
