\chapter{2005 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Robert Aumann (Israel), Thomas Schelling (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
Game theory analysis of conflict and cooperation

\section{What They Did}
Robert Aumann developed repeated game theory, demonstrating how cooperation can emerge in prisoner's dilemmas through patient, infinitely repeated interactions. He formalized the concept of common knowledge, analyzing how shared understanding enables coordination equilibria. Aumann also developed bargaining theory with solution concepts for cooperative games, and the Shapley value provides a fair division of surplus. He introduced correlated equilibrium, allowing players to coordinate through signals from a mediator without binding agreements. This generalizes Nash equilibrium, allowing richer coordination possibilities. Thomas Schelling applied game-theoretic insights to military strategy, bargaining, and conflict resolution. He developed understanding of commitment mechanisms and bargaining positions. His analysis of conflict escalation, deterrence, and crisis bargaining showed how strategic interaction shapes outcomes. Schelling distinguished between coercive diplomacy—threatening harm to compel adversary action—and signaling resolve through credible threats. Reputation built through repeated interactions reinforces commitment to positions.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Aumann and Schelling's contributions transformed game theory from abstract mathematics to practical analysis of human interaction. Cooperation can be sustained in repeated relationships where reputation matters; credible threats enable successful coercion and bargaining. Success depends on ability to make credible commitments through observable actions that constrain future options. Building reputation through repeated interactions establishes reliability, encouraging cooperation. The correlated equilibrium concept enriches strategic analysis, allowing mediators to facilitate coordination without explicit contracts. Common knowledge formalizes the coordination requirement for shared understanding of reasoning processes. Their strategic analysis extends to diplomacy, conflict resolution, arms control negotiations, and treaty compliance verification. International law incorporates game-theoretic insights about enforcement mechanisms, credible commitments, and reputation effects.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Repeated game theory extends to evolutionary biology, where replicator dynamics shows how cooperative strategies evolve under natural selection. Behavioral experiments validate Aumann's cooperation findings in lab settings. Schelling's focal points and salience help coordinate expectations in coordination games. Correlated equilibrium now facilitates coordination in traffic management, spectrum allocation, and auction design. Matching markets for school assignments, residency placements, and kidney transplants draw on these insights. Coalition formation, alliances, joint ventures, and partnerships all employ game-theoretic analysis. The framework informs industrial organization, labor negotiations, and corporate governance. As strategic interaction permeates economics, computer science, political science, and biology, Aumann and Schelling's theoretical foundations remain essential for understanding cooperation and conflict.
