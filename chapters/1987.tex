\chapter{1987 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Robert Solow (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his contributions to the theory of economic growth

\section{What They Did}
Robert Solow developed the neoclassical growth model, showing how capital
accumulation, labor, and technological progress interact in long-run growth.
Born in Brooklyn in 1924, Solow served in the U.S. Army during World War II
and later earned his doctorate at Harvard. He joined MIT in 1949 and spent the
rest of his academic career there.

His 1956 paper ``A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth'' introduced
the model commonly associated with Solow and Trevor Swan, who developed a
closely related model independently. The framework became a standard way to
analyze long-run growth. It shows that capital accumulation contributes to
output but faces diminishing returns; with exogenous technological progress,
long-run growth in output per worker depends on that progress rather than on
capital deepening alone.

Solow's 1957 growth-accounting analysis identified the residual portion of
output growth not explained by measured capital and labor inputs. The residual
is often interpreted as technological progress or total factor productivity,
but it also captures measurement error, omitted variables, utilization changes,
and model misspecification. The framework decomposes output growth into
contributions from capital, labor, and the residual; its quantitative findings
highlighted the importance of productivity growth in explaining growth in
many advanced economies.

His neoclassical growth model implies conditional convergence: economies with
lower initial capital per worker may grow faster than richer economies when
their saving, population growth, technology, and institutional conditions are
similar. This prediction guided empirical research on development and
convergence, but it does not imply unconditional convergence across all
countries.

Solow also wrote on labor markets, unemployment, inflation, and the
relationship between wages and productivity. These contributions informed
macroeconomic policy debates, but the Phillips curve and the natural-rate
hypothesis were developed by a wider group of economists.

Solow's work also contributed to the analysis of capital accumulation and the
measurement of productivity. Growth accounting requires difficult choices about
how to measure heterogeneous capital and labor inputs; later researchers
developed and refined capital-service measures used in applied productivity
analysis.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Solow helped transform growth economics into a quantitative field. He provided
a framework in which capital accumulation contributes to growth but diminishing
returns limit its long-run effect, while technological progress can sustain
growth in output per worker. The model itself does not establish that any
particular investment in R\&D or education will cause faster growth.

His identification of the residual attributed to technological change became a
standard growth-accounting procedure. Economists decompose output growth into
capital, labor, and productivity components using related methods. In many
advanced economies, these exercises assign a substantial share of long-run
growth to productivity, although the result depends on measurement and factor-
share assumptions.

The conditional-convergence implication helped organize research on
cross-country income differences. Poorer nations may catch up when saving,
technology, human capital, and institutions are sufficiently similar, while
empirical studies also find divergence and persistent differences between
economies.

His model clarified that saving and investment affect the level of income per
worker, while exogenous technological progress determines the long-run growth
rate in the baseline model. This distinction influenced debates about saving,
investment, innovation, and development, while later theories endogenized
some of the forces treated as external by Solow.

The Solow model provided an influential and transparent mathematical framework
for long-run growth, building on earlier work by economists such as Harrod and
Domar. Its simplicity made it a standard introductory tool and a benchmark for
more elaborate growth models.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Endogenous growth theories challenged Solow's treatment of technological
progress as exogenous. Romer and others modeled innovation, knowledge
spillovers, and human-capital accumulation as internal to the growth process,
allowing policy and incentives to affect long-run growth in those models. The
Solow model remains an important benchmark.

The model is widely taught in macroeconomics and remains a starting point for
empirical tests of convergence and for analysis of growth accounting. It helps
policymakers distinguish policies that change the level of output from forces
that can sustain long-run productivity growth, though its conclusions depend
on its assumptions.

Research extending the Solow model incorporates environmental sustainability,
demographic transition, and climate change. These applications preserve the
model's role as a benchmark while adding resource constraints, damages, and
non-market effects. In the baseline model, long-run per-worker growth requires
technological progress rather than capital accumulation alone; other models
modify that conclusion.

Growth accounting remains a standard tool for decomposing economic
performance. It informs debates about productivity slowdowns, the returns to
education, and the sources of growth. Recent research has applied related
methods to questions about digital technologies and artificial intelligence,
while recognizing the measurement challenges involved.
