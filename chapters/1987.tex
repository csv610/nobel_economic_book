\chapter{1987 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Robert Solow (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his contributions to the theory of economic growth

\section{What They Did}
Robert Solow developed the exogenous growth model showing capital accumulation
and technological progress drive long-run productivity growth. Born in Brooklyn
in 1924, Solow served in the U.S. Army during World War II before earning his
doctorate at Harvard under Paul Samuelson. He spent most of his career at
MIT, where he became one of the most influential economists of the twentieth
century.

His 1956 paper "A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth" introduced
the Solow-Swan growth model, which became the standard framework for
analyzing long-run economic growth. The model shows that capital accumulation
contributes to growth but faces diminishing returns. Eventually, an economy
reaches a steady state where per capita growth depends only on technological
progress.

Solow identified the "Solow residual"--the portion of output growth not
explained by growth in capital and labor inputs. This residual is attributed
to technological progress, though it also captures measurement errors,
omitted variables, and other factors. The growth accounting framework
decomposes output growth into contributions from capital, labor, and total
factor productivity.

His neoclassical growth model demonstrated conditional convergence: poorer
economies tend to grow faster than richer ones if they have similar saving
rates, population growth, and access to technology. This prediction has
guided empirical research on economic development and convergence.

Solow also contributed to labor economics, studying unemployment, inflation,
and the relationship between wages and productivity. His work on the
Phillips curve and natural rate of unemployment influenced monetary policy
debates.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Solow transformed growth economics from philosophical speculation into
quantitative analysis. He provided a framework where capital accumulation
contributes to growth but diminishing returns limit its effect. Technological
progress proved essential for sustained growth, explaining why countries
that invest more in R\&D and education grow faster over time.

His identification of the residual attributed to technological change became
the standard growth accounting procedure. Economists worldwide decompose
output growth into capital, labor, and productivity components using Solow's
methodology. This framework has revealed that most long-run growth in
advanced economies comes from technological progress, not capital accumulation.

The conditional convergence hypothesis explained patterns in cross-country
incomes. Poorer nations tend to grow faster, catching up with rich ones when
saving rates and institutions are similar. Empirical tests of convergence
have revealed both convergence and divergence patterns, leading to refined
theories about the conditions for catch-up growth.

His model demonstrated the importance of savings rates and investment in
capital formation. It also emphasized ultimate dependency on technological
advancement for long-run growth. This insight has influenced debates about
the role of savings, investment, and innovation in economic development.

The Solow model provided the first coherent framework for thinking about
long-run economic growth. It replaced earlier qualitative discussions with
a rigorous mathematical model. Its simplicity and transparency made it the
standard introductory tool for growth economics.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Endogenous growth theories emerged challenging Solow's exogenous
technological progress assumption. Romer and others internalized innovation,
knowledge spillovers, and human capital accumulation into growth models,
showing how policy can affect long-run growth rates. Yet the Solow model
remains the fundamental benchmark for growth analysis.

It is taught in introductory macroeconomics courses worldwide as the
foundation for understanding growth. Empirical studies testing convergence
hypotheses use the Solow framework as a starting point. Policy makers
evaluating growth strategies reference Solow conclusions about technological
progress.

Research extending the Solow model incorporates environmental sustainability,
demographic transition, and climate change. These reflections demonstrate
Solow's enduring relevance. The fundamental insight that long-run growth
depends on technological progress rather than capital accumulation alone
remains uncontroversial.

The growth accounting methodology he developed remains the standard tool
for decomposing economic performance. It continues to inform debates about
productivity slowdowns, the returns to education, and the sources of
economic growth in the twenty-first century.
