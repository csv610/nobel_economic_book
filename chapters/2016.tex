\chapter{2016 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Oliver Hart (USA/UK), Bengt Holmström (Finland)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their contributions to contract theory

\section{What They Did}
Oliver Hart and Bengt Holmström advanced contract theory, providing insights
into how contractual arrangements should be designed to align incentives when
parties have different information and objectives.

Hart developed the incomplete contracts approach, showing that because contracts
cannot specify all future contingencies, property rights and control rights
matter. He analyzed how ownership structure affects investment incentives and
firm boundaries. His work explained why some activities are performed within
firms while others are outsourced, providing a theoretical foundation for the
make-or-buy decision.

Hart's analysis of property rights showed that ownership determines who has the
residual right to make decisions about an asset when contracts are incomplete.
This insight has important implications for mergers, joint ventures, and
public-private partnerships. He demonstrated that the optimal allocation of
ownership depends on the relative importance of relationship-specific investments
by each party. His theory of the firm explained how these control rights help
determine organizational boundaries and investment incentives.

Holmström developed the principal-agent framework, showing how optimal contracts
balance incentives with insurance when effort is unobservable. His work on
moral hazard demonstrated that when agents' actions cannot be perfectly monitored,
optimal contracts must trade off risk-sharing against incentive provision.
He showed how performance measures, pay-for-performance schemes, and career
concerns affect agent behavior.

In dynamic incentive problems, his analyses also showed how costly actions can
sometimes make threats or promises credible. His
work on team production explained why individual performance pay may be weakened
when workers can free-ride on one another and how compensation can balance
individual incentives with group objectives.

Together, they helped create a general framework for analyzing contractual
relationships in firms, markets, and organizations. Their complementary
approaches---Hart focusing on property rights and Holmström on incentive
contracts---provided tools for analyzing organizational design.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Hart and Holmström transformed understanding of contractual relationships and
organizational design. Their work explained why firms have boundaries, how
ownership affects investment incentives, and why contracts are often incomplete.

This influenced corporate governance, executive compensation, and merger analysis.
The incomplete-contracts framework provides tools for analyzing public-private
partnerships, outsourcing decisions, and joint ventures. It also helps
policymakers think about procurement contracts and the governance of regulated
industries.

Holmström's incentive theory informed compensation design in corporations,
nonprofits, and public sector organizations. His work on executive pay has
influenced debates about the appropriate role of stock options and performance
bonuses in aligning manager and shareholder interests.

Hart's work on property rights clarified debates about privatization, regulation,
and institutional design. His framework has been applied to questions involving
utility regulation, healthcare provision, and environmental policy, showing how
ownership and control rights can affect investment and efficiency.

Their combined contributions made contract theory a central part of modern
economics, with applications spanning industrial organization, labor economics,
public economics, and corporate finance. The theory has also influenced legal
thinking about contracts, property rights, and corporate governance.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Contract theory remains important for understanding modern economic organization.
Hart's incomplete-contracts framework informs debates about corporate governance,
private equity, and firm boundaries. Recent work applies related ideas to platform
economies, examining how control over data, algorithms, and infrastructure can
affect market power.

Holmström's incentive theory guides analysis of compensation design, including
stock options, performance bonuses, and executive pay. Research on algorithmic
management examines how performance-monitoring systems affect worker behavior and
welfare in gig-economy platforms. Related contract-theoretic work is being
extended to questions about artificial intelligence and machine-learning
incentives.

The theory also informs labor market analysis, including gig economy arrangements
and remote work contracts. Public policy debates about healthcare provision,
education outsourcing, and infrastructure development draw on contract theory
insights about incentive compatibility and risk sharing.

As work arrangements evolve with technology and globalization, Hart and
Holmström's theoretical tools remain useful for analyzing incentive compatibility
and organizational design. Their work continues to inform research on artificial-
intelligence governance, data property rights, and the future of work in digital
economies.
