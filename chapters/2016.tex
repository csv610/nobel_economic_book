\chapter{2016 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Oliver Hart (USA/UK), Bengt Holmström (Finland)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their contributions to contract theory

\section{What They Did}
Oliver Hart and Bengt Holmström advanced contract theory, providing insights
into how contractual arrangements should be designed to align incentives when
parties have different information and objectives.

Hart developed the incomplete contracts approach, showing that because contracts
cannot specify all future contingencies, property rights and control rights
matter. He analyzed how ownership structure affects investment incentives and
firm boundaries. His work explained why some activities are performed within
firms while others are outsourced, providing a theoretical foundation for the
make-or-buy decision.

Hart's analysis of property rights showed that ownership determines who has the
right to make decisions about an asset when contracts are incomplete. This
insight has important implications for mergers, joint ventures, and public-private
partnerships. He demonstrated that the optimal allocation of ownership depends
on the relative importance of relationship-specific investments by each party.

Holmström developed the principal-agent framework, showing how optimal contracts
balance incentives with insurance when effort is unobservable. His work on
moral hazard demonstrated that when agents' actions cannot be perfectly monitored,
optimal contracts must trade off risk-sharing against incentive provision.

He analyzed how performance measures, pay-for-performance schemes, and career
concerns affect agent behavior. Holmström's "burning money" principle showed
that destroying resources can improve incentives by making threats more credible.
His work on team production explained how to design compensation systems that
align individual incentives with group objectives.

Together, they created a comprehensive theory of contractual relationships in
firms, markets, and organizations. Their complementary approaches--Hart focusing
 on property rights and Holmström on incentive contracts--provided a unified
framework for analyzing organizational design.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Hart and Holmström transformed understanding of contractual relationships and
organizational design. Their work explained why firms have boundaries, how
ownership affects investment incentives, and why contracts are often incomplete.

This influenced corporate governance, executive compensation, and merger analysis.
The incomplete contracts framework provided tools for analyzing public-private
partnerships, outsourcing decisions, and joint ventures. Policymakers use these
insights to design better procurement contracts and regulate natural monopolies.

Holmström's incentive theory informed compensation design in corporations,
nonprofits, and public sector organizations. His work on executive pay has
influenced debates about the appropriate role of stock options and performance
bonuses in aligning manager and shareholder interests.

Hart's work on property rights clarified debates about privatization, regulation,
and institutional design. His analysis has been applied to utility regulation,
healthcare provision, and environmental policy, showing how ownership and
control rights affect investment and efficiency.

Their combined contributions made contract theory a central pillar of modern
economics, with applications spanning industrial organization, labor economics,
public economics, and corporate finance. The theory has influenced legal
thinking about contracts, property rights, and corporate governance.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Contract theory remains essential for understanding modern economic organization.
Hart's incomplete contracts framework informs debates about corporate governance,
private equity, and firm boundaries. Recent work applies these insights to
platform economies, examining how ownership of data and algorithms affects
market power.

Holmström's incentive theory guides compensation design in the age of stock
options, performance bonuses, and executive pay. Research on algorithmic
management examines how performance monitoring systems affect worker behavior
and welfare in gig economy platforms.

The theory also informs labor market analysis, including gig economy arrangements
and remote work contracts. Public policy debates about healthcare provision,
education outsourcing, and infrastructure development draw on contract theory
insights about incentive compatibility and risk sharing.

As work arrangements evolve with technology and globalization, Hart and
Holmström's theoretical tools remain indispensable for analyzing incentive
compatibility and organizational design. Their work continues to influence
research on artificial intelligence governance, data property rights, and the
future of work in digital economies.
