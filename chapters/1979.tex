\chapter{1979 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Theodore Schultz (USA), Arthur Lewis (UK)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their pioneering research into economic development research with particular significance for the problems of developing countries

\section{What They Did}
Theodore Schultz emphasized the importance of human capital in agriculture and development in poor countries, arguing that investment in education and health is as productive as investment in physical capital. He demonstrated that returns to human capital investment often exceed returns to physical capital in developing economies. Arthur Lewis created the dual-sector model of economic development, explaining structural transformation from low-productivity agriculture to higher-productivity industry through migration of surplus labor to urban areas. Lewis's model described the transition of a developing economy from agricultural surplus to wage equalization and industrialization driven by migration. He showed how growing industrial sectors absorb agricultural surplus labor at constant wages until the turning point where scarcity drives wages up. Their combined work shifted development economics from focusing solely on physical capital accumulation to recognizing the critical roles of human capabilities and structural transformation.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Their work shifted focus from merely physical capital accumulation to human capabilities and structural change, laying the foundation for modern development economics. Schultz's emphasis on human capital informed agricultural investment, rural development policies, and industrialization strategies. He argued that investments in education and health are as productive as investments in machinery and infrastructure. Lewis's analysis described how growing industrial sectors absorb agricultural surplus, creating a self-reinforcing development cycle. Their combined work justified targeted development interventions, recognizing agriculture as the critical sector in early development. The human capital concept became central to growth theory, explaining cross-country income differences. \section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Human capital theory has become central to education policy, labor economics, and growth theory worldwide. Lewis's dual-sector model continues informing structural transformation analysis and development indicators. Modern development indicators incorporate human development and capability approaches, adding gender dimensions to Schultz's original insights. Dual-process models continue informing policy regarding rural-urban migration and industrialization sequencing. Human capital returns estimation guides education spending decisions in developing countries. Understanding the complementary roles of agriculture and industry remains relevant today, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia undergoing similar transitions. Schultz's work on agricultural development informs contemporary debates about food security and rural transformation. Lewis's migration framework continues explaining urbanization patterns in developing economies. Their combined legacy endures in how economists understand the foundations of economic development.
