\chapter{2000 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }James Heckman (USA), Daniel McFadden (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For having developed methods for analyzing selective samples and discrete choices

\section{What They Did}
James Heckman and Daniel McFadden were honored for pioneering contributions to
the econometric analysis of microdata that address two fundamental and pervasive
problems in empirical research: selection bias and discrete decision-making.

Heckman developed what has become known as the "Heckman correction" (also
called the inverse Mills ratio method) to address sample selection bias. The
problem arises when researchers observe outcomes only for a non-randomly
selected subsample of the population. A classic example is the study of wages:
researchers typically observe wages only for individuals who are employed, not
for those who have chosen not to participate in the labor force. When the
decision to participate is correlated with unobserved factors that also affect
wages, standard ordinary least squares regression produces biased and
inconsistent estimates.

Heckman showed how to correct this by estimating a two-stage procedure. First,
a probit selection equation determines the probability of being included in the
sample. Second, the inverse Mills ratio (also called the Heckman lambda) is
included as an additional regressor in the outcome equation. This correction
accounts for the correlation between the error terms of the selection and
outcome equations, yielding consistent estimates.

Beyond sample selection, Heckman made profound contributions to the evaluation
of social programs. He developed methods for estimating treatment effects from
non-experimental data, recognizing that randomized controlled trials are not
always feasible or ethical. His work on lifecycle models of skill formation
showed how early interventions yield higher returns than later ones,
fundamentally changing debates about the economics of education and human
capital.

McFadden developed the econometric theory of discrete choice models. When
individuals choose among a finite set of alternatives--such as modes of
transportation, employers, neighborhoods, or consumer products--their choices
can be modeled using random utility theory. McFadden showed that the multinomial
logit model arises naturally from assuming that the unobserved components of
utility are independently and identically distributed according to a Type I
extreme value distribution.

This elegant result made discrete choice modeling computationally tractable
while remaining empirically flexible. He established the statistical properties
of maximum likelihood estimation in this context, including consistency,
asymptotic normality, and the likelihood ratio test. The conditional logit
model extended these ideas to alternatives described by observer-specific
attributes. Later work on the mixed logit model accommodated correlation across
alternatives and unobserved heterogeneity. McFadden's 1974 paper on the
conditional logit model and his 1981 econometrics textbook became definitive
references in the field.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
The Heckman correction eliminated a persistent and often invisible source of
bias in labor economics, health economics, education research, and program
evaluation. Prior to Heckman's work, many studies of wage determination, labor
force participation, and the effects of social programs produced misleading
estimates because they failed to account for the fact that individuals
self-select into observable outcomes.

McFadden's discrete choice models became the foundation of empirical work on
consumer behavior, transportation planning, marketing research, and
environmental valuation. The logit model, in particular, proved remarkably
tractable while capturing essential features of choice behavior. McFadden's
work provided the statistical machinery for estimating choice models under a
wide range of assumptions.

Together, their methods enabled researchers to evaluate policy programs, assess
discrimination, and forecast demand with greater credibility. The instrumental
variables, regression discontinuity, and difference-in-differences approaches
that followed built directly on Heckman's insights about identification in
non-experimental settings.

Heckman's lifecycle model of skill formation, developed in collaboration with
others, showed that the returns to investment in human capital are highest in
early childhood and decline over the life course. This finding had profound
implications for education policy, suggesting that early interventions such as
preschool programs yield greater social returns than remedial programs targeted
at older children or adults.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Heckman and McFadden's methodologies are now ubiquitous across the social
sciences. In labor economics, Heckman's work on selection bias and treatment
effects remains essential for evaluating employment training programs, minimum
wage policies, and the returns to education.

Discrete choice models inform transportation policy, housing market analysis,
environmental valuation, and marketing strategy. They are used to estimate
willingness-to-pay for policy changes, forecast travel demand under different
infrastructure scenarios, and design auction mechanisms for spectrum allocation.
Health economists use discrete choice experiments to elicit patient preferences
for treatment options and healthcare attributes.

Machine learning approaches have extended Heckman-McFadden methods to
high-dimensional settings, while causal inference frameworks such as causal
forests and double machine learning continue to build on Heckman's
identification strategies. Their combined legacy endures as the backbone of
modern empirical microeconomics.
