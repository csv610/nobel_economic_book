\chapter{2015 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Angus Deaton (UK/USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his analysis of consumption, poverty, and welfare

\section{What They Did}
Angus Deaton made groundbreaking contributions to the analysis of consumption,
poverty, and welfare. Born in Scotland in 1945, Deaton earned his doctorate
from Cambridge University before becoming a professor at Princeton University.

He developed the almost ideal demand system (AIDS), a flexible functional form
for estimating consumer demand that became standard in empirical industrial
organization and policy analysis. The AIDS model allows researchers to estimate
how consumers respond to price changes and income variations while satisfying
theoretical restrictions like symmetry and adding-up. This framework has been
used to evaluate tax policies, analyze market structures, and assess the
distributional effects of economic reforms.

Deaton's work on household survey methodology improved how economists measure
poverty and living standards in developing countries. He argued that consumption
measures are often superior to income measures for assessing welfare, particularly
in rural areas where income is volatile and difficult to measure accurately.
His research on survey design and measurement error has improved the quality
of data used in poverty analysis worldwide.

He analyzed the relationship between consumption, income, and wealth, showing
how permanent income fluctuations affect current consumption. Deaton's work
on consumption smoothing demonstrated how households use saving, borrowing,
and social networks to manage risk and maintain stable consumption despite
income volatility.

His research on health economics examined how health shocks affect consumption
patterns and poverty dynamics. Deaton showed that health and economic well-being
are deeply intertwined, with health improvements enabling economic participation
and economic resources enabling better health outcomes. His work on the
macroeconomics of health connected individual health decisions to aggregate
economic outcomes.

Deaton also studied the political economy of development, analyzing how
government policies affect poverty reduction and welfare. His research on
South Africa examined the relationship between apartheid, inequality, and
health outcomes, providing insights into how political institutions shape
economic and health outcomes.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Deaton transformed consumption theory and poverty measurement. The almost ideal
demand system provided researchers with practical tools for analyzing consumer
behavior and evaluating policy impacts. His work on household surveys established
standards for collecting and analyzing microdata in developing countries.

Deaton's analysis of consumption smoothing showed how households manage risk
through saving, borrowing, and social networks. His research on the macroeconomics
of health connected individual health decisions to aggregate economic outcomes,
influencing debates about health insurance, preventive care, and public health
spending.

Deaton's later work on inequality examined how wealth and income distributions
affect political institutions and economic performance. His book "The Great
Escape" (2013) argued that health improvements have done more for human welfare
than income growth alone, challenging the conventional focus on GDP as the
primary measure of progress.

His interdisciplinary approach enriched economics, demography, and public health.
He showed how economic methods can address fundamental questions about human
well-being, bridging the gap between quantitative analysis and qualitative
understanding of human experience.

Deaton's work on measurement has influenced international poverty estimates and
the design of social protection programs. His emphasis on high-quality microdata
has raised standards for empirical research in development economics.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Deaton's methodological contributions remain central to empirical economics. The
almost ideal demand system is widely used in consumer research, policy evaluation,
and industrial organization. His poverty measurement frameworks inform international
development policy and World Bank analyses.

Research on consumption smoothing continues to explore how financial development,
social insurance, and credit constraints affect household welfare. Deaton's work
on health economics influences health policy debates about insurance coverage,
preventive care, and health inequality. His insights about the relationship
between health and economic outcomes have informed pandemic response strategies.

His recent research on inequality and democracy has sparked debates about the
relationship between economic concentration and political power. Deaton has
argued that rising inequality undermines democratic governance and social
cohesion, and that policies addressing inequality require understanding both
economic and political mechanisms.

As global inequality and health disparities persist, Deaton's analytical tools
remain essential for understanding and addressing these challenges. His emphasis
on measurement quality and interdisciplinary approaches continues to guide
research on poverty, health, and well-being in the twenty-first century.
