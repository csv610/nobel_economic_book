\chapter{1990 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Harry Markowitz (USA), Merton Miller (USA), William Sharpe (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
Portfolio theory, derivatives pricing, and capital structure theory

\section{What They Did}
Harry Markowitz created modern portfolio theory and mean-variance optimization,
establishing the principle that diversification reduces unsystematic risk through
the combination of assets. His quadratic optimization approach selects portfolios
on the efficient frontier, maximizing return for a given level of risk or
minimizing risk for a given level of return. Markowitz showed that investors
should not evaluate assets in isolation but rather consider how each asset
contributes to the overall portfolio risk and return.

William Sharpe developed the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), relating
expected returns to systematic risk through the beta coefficient. Sharpe showed
that in equilibrium, the only risk that earns a premium is non-diversifiable
market risk. The CAPM provides a simple, elegant framework for pricing risky
assets: expected return equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the market
risk premium. The model reduces the efficient frontier to a straight line
representing the tradeoff between risk and return.

Merton Miller developed the Modigliani-Miller theorem on capital structure
irrelevance, showing that in perfect markets, a firm's value is independent of
its financing decisions between debt and equity. The MM theorem laid the
foundation for corporate finance by revealing that financing decisions do not
affect firm value under idealized conditions. Miller later extended the theorem
to show how taxes, bankruptcy costs, and agency costs determine optimal capital
structure, creating the trade-off theory of capital structure.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Markowitz revolutionized investment management by providing a systematic
framework for constructing portfolios that balance risk and return. His work
established the modern practice of financial advisory, asset management, mutual
funds, and pension plan design. The concept of diversification became the
cornerstone of investment theory, replacing the naive approach of selecting
individual securities based on standalone merits.

Sharpe's CAPM became the standard tool for evaluating investment performance
and measuring risk-adjusted returns. The alpha and beta decomposition derived
directly from CAPM, providing benchmarks for performance evaluation. Institutional
investors use CAPM to determine required rates of return for capital budgeting
decisions. The model's simplicity and empirical tractability made it the most
influential equation in financial economics.

Miller's MM theorem initiated the corporate finance research program examining
how financing decisions affect firm value. By establishing a clean baseline where
financing is irrelevant, Miller provided the framework for analyzing real-world
deviations caused by taxes, bankruptcy costs, information asymmetries, and agency
problems. The trade-off theory emerged from extending MM to incorporate tax
shields from debt and the costs of financial distress.

Their combined contributions transformed financial economics from a descriptive
collection of observations into a rigorous theoretical discipline. The
Markowitz-Sharpe-CAPM framework underpins modern portfolio theory, while the
MM theorem serves as the fundamental building block of corporate finance.
Capital structure, corporate governance, stock options, and derivative hedging
all rest on the foundations laid by these three laureates.

\subsection*{The Efficient Frontier}
Markowitz demonstrated that rational investors should construct portfolios lying
on the efficient frontier, where no portfolio offers higher expected return for
the same level of risk. The frontier depends on the correlation structure among
asset returns, showing that low or negative correlations provide greater
diversification benefits. This insight explains why investors hold diversified
portfolios rather than concentrating in individual securities.

\subsection*{Beta and Risk Premium}
Sharpe's beta measures an asset's sensitivity to market movements, capturing
the portion of risk that cannot be eliminated through diversification. The CAPM
implies that expected returns are linearly related to beta, providing a clear
prescription for required returns on risky investments. This relationship
became the basis for calculating cost of capital in corporate finance.

\subsection*{Tax Shield and Capital Structure}
Miller's extension of the MM theorem incorporating corporate taxes showed that
debt financing creates a tax shield because interest payments are deductible.
This finding explains why firms typically carry debt and why the value of a
levered firm exceeds that of an unlevered firm. The trade-off between tax
benefits and bankruptcy costs determines optimal leverage.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Markowitz mean-variance optimization remains the foundation of Modern Portfolio
Theory, expanded to incorporate higher moments, alternative risk measures, and
downside risk concepts like Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall. Portfolio
construction algorithms now handle thousands of assets with transaction costs,
short-selling constraints, and liquidity considerations.

Sharpe's alpha and beta decomposition remains central to performance evaluation
of mutual funds, hedge funds, and pension plans. Beta factors underpin factor
investing and style rotation strategies. The CAPM has been modified into
multi-factor models, with Fama and French developing three-factor and five-factor
models that capture size, value, profitability, and investment dimensions beyond
market beta. These extensions address empirical anomalies while preserving the
core Sharpe insight that systematic risk commands a premium.

Miller's MM theorem remains the core of corporate finance teaching. Debt-equity
leverage decisions are analyzed alongside profitability and investment decisions
using MM as the theoretical benchmark. The theorem provides the framework against
which real-world imperfections are measured. Corporate governance codes, capital
structure regulations, and tax policy all interact with MM insights.

Investment advisors apply Markowitz diversification principles daily in portfolio
construction. Risk managers implement VaR and Expected Shortfall measures based
on Sharpe's insights about systematic versus idiosyncratic risk. Corporate
treasurers make financing decisions informed by MM theorem extensions that
incorporate taxes, agency costs, and financial distress. Each year, thousands
of new papers extend the Markowitz-Sharpe-Miller frameworks, and their work
remains a vibrant research frontier. Financial education centers around their
contributions, with every finance student learning their names as founders of
modern financial economics.
