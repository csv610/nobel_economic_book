\chapter{1990 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Harry Markowitz (USA), Merton Miller (USA), William Sharpe (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their pioneering work in the theory of financial economics

\section{What They Did}
Harry Markowitz developed modern portfolio theory and mean--variance analysis,
formalizing how diversification can reduce risk when asset returns are not
perfectly positively correlated. His quadratic optimization approach identifies
efficient portfolios that maximize expected return for a given variance or
minimize variance for a given expected return, under the model's assumptions.
Markowitz showed that an asset should be evaluated by its contribution to the
overall portfolio's risk and return, not in isolation.

William Sharpe developed a leading version of the Capital Asset Pricing Model
(CAPM), relating expected returns to systematic risk through the beta
coefficient. Under its equilibrium assumptions, the model implies that
idiosyncratic risk is not priced and that expected return equals the risk-free
rate plus beta times the market risk premium. The CAPM's security market line
is distinct from Markowitz's efficient frontier and from the capital market
line that combines the risk-free asset with the tangency portfolio.

Merton Miller, initially with Franco Modigliani, developed the
Modigliani--Miller propositions on corporate finance. Under idealized market
conditions, a firm's value is independent of its mix of debt and equity, and
dividend policy does not determine value. These propositions supplied a
benchmark for analyzing how taxes, financial distress, information, and agency
problems can make financing decisions matter. Later researchers developed the
trade-off and other theories of capital structure.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Markowitz provided investment management with a systematic framework for
constructing portfolios that balance expected return and risk. His work
influenced financial advice, asset management, mutual funds, and pension-plan
design. The concept of diversification became central to investment theory,
replacing the practice of judging securities only by their standalone
characteristics.

Sharpe's CAPM became an important benchmark for evaluating risk and expected
returns. Alpha and beta are commonly used in performance analysis, with beta
closely related to the CAPM's market-risk coefficient, although empirical
performance measures need not validate the model. Institutional investors and
firms use CAPM-based estimates of required returns alongside other models.

The Modigliani--Miller propositions initiated a corporate-finance research
program examining when financing decisions affect firm value. By establishing
a baseline in which financing is irrelevant, Miller and Modigliani provided a
framework for analyzing deviations caused by taxes, financial distress,
information asymmetries, and agency problems. Later capital-structure theories
study the balance between tax benefits and the costs of financial distress.

Their contributions helped establish financial economics as a rigorous
theoretical and empirical discipline. Markowitz's portfolio framework and
Sharpe's asset-pricing work underpin important parts of modern finance, while
the Modigliani--Miller propositions remain a benchmark in corporate finance.
Later work on capital structure, governance, securities, and derivatives
builds on these foundations but also introduces additional assumptions and
methods.

\subsection*{The Efficient Frontier}
Markowitz demonstrated that, within a mean--variance model, efficient
portfolios are those for which no portfolio offers higher expected return at
the same variance. The frontier depends on the covariance structure among
asset returns: low or negative correlations can create larger diversification
benefits. The result explains the logic of diversification without claiming
that every investor has mean--variance preferences.

\subsection*{Beta and Risk Premium}
Sharpe's beta measures an asset's covariance-based sensitivity to the market
portfolio, and in the CAPM it captures the component of risk that earns an
expected-return premium. The model implies a linear relation between expected
returns and beta, which became one basis for estimating the cost of capital,
although practitioners also use other models and estimates.

\subsection*{Tax Shield and Capital Structure}
Extensions of the MM propositions incorporating corporate taxes show that
interest deductibility can create a tax shield. In the tax-only version, debt
can increase firm value relative to an unlevered firm; financial distress,
personal taxes, information, and agency costs can offset that benefit. The
trade-off between these forces is one approach to analyzing leverage.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Markowitz mean--variance optimization remains a foundation of Modern Portfolio
Theory. Later methods incorporate higher moments, alternative risk measures,
and downside measures such as Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall. Portfolio
construction algorithms now incorporate transaction costs, short-selling
constraints, liquidity, and large numbers of assets.

Sharpe's alpha and beta remain widely used in performance evaluation of mutual
funds, hedge funds, and pension portfolios. The CAPM has also been extended by
multi-factor models, including the Fama--French models, which add factors such
as size, value, profitability, and investment. These models address empirical
limitations of a single market factor and do not preserve every CAPM
implication.

The Modigliani--Miller propositions remain a central benchmark in corporate
finance teaching. Debt--equity decisions are analyzed alongside investment and
profitability using the idealized theorem as a reference point. Corporate
governance, capital-structure regulation, and tax policy interact with the
market imperfections that the benchmark abstracts from.

Investment advisers apply diversification principles in portfolio construction,
while risk managers use a range of risk measures, including VaR and Expected
Shortfall. Corporate treasurers make financing decisions informed by
extensions of the Modigliani--Miller benchmark that incorporate taxes, agency
costs, and financial distress. The three laureates' frameworks remain active
research areas and foundational topics in financial economics.
