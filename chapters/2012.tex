\chapter{2012 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Alvin Roth (USA), Lloyd Shapley (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design

\section{What They Did}
Lloyd Shapley and David Gale developed the theory of stable matching and the
deferred-acceptance algorithm for two-sided markets. They proved that a stable
matching exists under the model's preference assumptions and gave an algorithm
that finds one.

Their work showed that the deferred-acceptance algorithm produces a matching
that is optimal for the proposing side among stable matchings and is strategy-
proof for that side. The allocation may systematically favor one side over the
other, depending on which side proposes. Roth later showed how this theory could
be applied to practical market-design problems.

They analyzed matching markets for medical residents and school-choice programs,
while Roth and later researchers extended related ideas to kidney exchanges.
Roth identified when existing institutions failed and required design
intervention, developing the empirical study of matching markets and showing
that some mechanisms cannot be both stable and strategy-proof for every
participant.

Shapley's earlier work on cooperative game theory and the Shapley value provided
the theoretical foundation for understanding fair allocation in collaborative
settings. The Shapley value assigns each player their average marginal
contribution across all possible orders in which a coalition can form, providing
a principled method for dividing a cooperative surplus among participants.

Roth recognized that the market for medical residents could be improved. The
earlier system suffered from instability and unraveling as hospitals and students
made offers before the main clearinghouse, encouraging strategic behavior and
early contracting. Roth and Elliott Peranson helped redesign the mechanism used
by the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP), which adopted a version of
deferred acceptance in 1998.

The theory also applies to school-choice systems. Economists including Atila
Abdulkadiroglu, Parag Pathak, and Roth helped redesign the Boston Public Schools
mechanism, which had encouraged strategic manipulation. The system was replaced
with a student-proposing deferred-acceptance mechanism, improving truthful
participation and the stability properties of the assignment.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Shapley and Roth transformed understanding of market design and matching theory.
Their work showed that the rules governing many markets can be designed and
improved rather than treated as given. The stable matching framework provided
tools for analyzing allocation problems where prices are prohibited or impractical.

This influenced public policy regarding school choice, organ transplantation,
and housing allocation. Roth's practical interventions demonstrated how economic
theory, empirical evidence, and institutional knowledge can be combined to improve
matching mechanisms. The redesign of the Boston school-choice system showed how
theoretical insights could be applied to a real-world allocation problem.

The work complemented price theory by showing that many important allocations
occur without prices. Labor markets, housing markets, and educational placements
often cannot use prices because of legal, ethical, or practical constraints.
Stable matching provides a theoretical foundation for some of these non-price
allocation mechanisms.

Their theory also influenced computer science, game theory, and operations
research. The algorithmic foundations they established provided computational
tools for solving matching problems in practice, with applications ranging from
school choice and kidney exchange to other allocation settings.

The concept of stability has become central to matching theory and important in
related areas of mechanism design, providing a benchmark for evaluating some
allocation mechanisms. Researchers analyze whether proposed mechanisms produce
stable outcomes and how they perform under strategic behavior.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Market design based on Shapley and Roth's work has expanded to diverse
applications. School-choice systems in New York, Boston, and other cities use
deferred-acceptance mechanisms to assign students to schools, with outcomes that
depend on the priorities, capacities, and objectives built into each system.

Kidney-exchange programs match incompatible donor--patient pairs across hospitals.
These programs generally use graph algorithms and optimization to form feasible
cycles and chains, drawing on market-design principles rather than applying the
standard two-sided stable-matching algorithm directly. Roth has advocated for
expanded use of such programs to address the shortage of kidneys for transplant.

Spectrum auctions, blood-donation systems, and some dating algorithms draw on
broader ideas from market design and matching theory. The Federal Communications
Commission has used auction designs to allocate radio frequencies, although these
auctions are allocation mechanisms rather than standard stable-matching markets.

Roth has advocated for market design solutions to organ shortage crises,
proposing paired exchange and priority markets. The theory has been extended to
dynamic matching, heterogeneous agents, and network-based allocations.

Digital platforms like ride-sharing and food delivery use real-time matching and
optimization algorithms to balance supply and demand across geographic spaces.
These systems need not satisfy the stability or incentive properties of deferred
acceptance. Climate-policy mechanisms, such as carbon-credit trading, likewise
draw on broader market-design insights when allocating or pricing emissions.

As matching theory continues to evolve, researchers apply it to emerging
challenges such as organ allocation, school choice in the age of virtual
learning, and resource allocation in digital marketplaces.
