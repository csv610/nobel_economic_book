\chapter{1997 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Robert Merton (USA), Myron Scholes (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For a new method to determine the value of derivatives

\section{What They Did}
Robert Merton and Myron Scholes developed a method for valuing derivatives,
including the Black--Scholes formula for European options. In its simplest form,
the model assumes a lognormally distributed stock price, constant volatility,
no dividends, frictionless markets, and continuous trading. The associated
risk-neutral valuation method transforms the pricing problem into the discounted
expected payoff under an equivalent pricing measure; it does not assume that
investors are actually risk-neutral.

The Black--Scholes framework provides a benchmark for pricing options and for
constructing hedges. Its assumptions are idealized, so market prices are often
compared through implied volatility and adjusted with richer models. Merton made
broader contributions to continuous-time finance, including option-pricing work
with dividends and important methods for valuing contingent claims; later
research extended the framework to American options and stochastic volatility.

Fischer Black, who died before the award, was a co-author of the original model
and made an essential contribution to its development. Their work helped provide
the analytical foundation for the expansion of organized and over-the-counter
derivatives markets, although market growth also depended on institutions,
technology, regulation, and later research.

Merton contributed substantially to continuous-time finance, applying stochastic
calculus to portfolio choice, dynamic hedging, and the pricing of contingent
claims. The Black--Scholes--Merton framework became an important foundation for
financial engineering and for the design of derivatives. A portfolio-insurance
strategy can seek to replicate a put option dynamically, but such protection is
subject to model error, transaction costs, jumps, and liquidity constraints.

\subsection*{The Black--Scholes Formula}
Under the simplest assumptions, the Black--Scholes formula prices a European call
option as:
$C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)$
where $S_0$ is the current stock price, $K$ is the strike price, $r$ is the risk-free
rate, $T$ is time to expiration, $N$ is the cumulative normal distribution, and
$d_1$ and $d_2$ depend on volatility and the other parameters. The formula uses
the stock price, strike price, time to expiration, risk-free rate, and volatility;
volatility is generally estimated or inferred from market prices rather than
directly observed.

\subsection*{Risk-Neutral Valuation}
Merton and Scholes helped establish risk-neutral valuation, in which prices can
be computed using an equivalent pricing measure under which suitably discounted
asset prices are martingales. This is a pricing device, not a claim that investors
are risk-neutral. Under the model, the physical expected return on the underlying
asset does not enter the option-pricing formula; the risk-free rate, volatility,
and other contract terms do. The method became a central tool for pricing many
derivatives, subject to model and market assumptions.

\subsection*{Dynamic Hedging}
The Black--Scholes framework motivates delta hedging, in which options dealers
adjust their stock holdings as the underlying price changes. An option's delta
measures its local sensitivity to the underlying price. In the idealized
continuous-trading model, holding the appropriate delta position eliminates the
instantaneous diffusion risk and the hedged portfolio earns the risk-free rate.
In practice, discrete trading, transaction costs, jumps, and parameter error
leave residual risk.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
The Black--Scholes--Merton framework changed financial economics by providing a
standardized way to value and hedge many option contracts. It supported the
development of structured products and hybrid securities, while the pricing of
each instrument still depends on its payoff, market, and model assumptions.

Risk-management techniques use delta, gamma, and vega sensitivities to monitor
exposure to price and volatility movements. Insurers may use option-pricing
methods to value embedded guarantees, and banks use derivatives-pricing models
alongside broader risk and capital frameworks. Value-at-Risk is a separate risk-
measurement framework, although it can use volatility estimates and distributions
informed by option markets.

Derivatives became important tools in corporate finance and treasury management.
Investment strategies use options for hedging, speculation, arbitrage, and risk
transfer. Option-pricing theory also informed real-options analysis for capital
budgeting, where investment timing and other forms of managerial flexibility have
value. Financial engineering subsequently developed structured products and
securitization instruments using many theories and technologies.

The 1998 Long-Term Capital Management crisis, in which Merton and Scholes were
involved, highlighted the dangers of leverage, liquidity risk, model error, and
correlated market moves that are not captured by simple assumptions. These events
did not invalidate the framework's analytical contribution, but they underscored
that model-based prices and hedges are conditional on their assumptions. Modern
derivatives markets support risk transfer and price discovery, while also
creating counterparty, leverage, and systemic risks.

\subsection*{Financial Engineering}
The Black--Scholes model became one of the foundations of financial engineering,
where quantitative methods are applied to financial problems. Derivatives
structuring, hedging, and risk management draw on its ideas alongside portfolio
theory, numerical methods, empirical modeling, and institutional knowledge.
Financial engineers create customized securities with risk exposures tailored to
particular clients and constraints.

\subsection*{Real Options}
Option-pricing ideas extend to real-options analysis, which applies the logic of
contingent claims to investment decisions under uncertainty. Real-options analysis
values managerial flexibility in capital budgeting, treating investment
opportunities as options.
The option to delay, expand, contract, or abandon projects has value that standard
DCF analysis misses. Real options have applications in R\&D, natural resources,
and strategic investment.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Option-pricing theory continues to evolve from the Black--Scholes foundation.
Stochastic-volatility models such as Heston's, local-volatility models, and
jump-diffusion models address limitations of constant volatility. GARCH models
are used mainly for time-series volatility forecasting, while implied-volatility
surfaces summarize the variation in market prices across strikes and maturities.
Volatility smiles and skews show that the constant-volatility assumption is an
imperfect description of observed prices.

Jump-diffusion processes incorporate discontinuous price movements and can improve
pricing for assets prone to sudden changes. Real-options analysis extends the
contingent-claims approach to capital budgeting, valuing sequential investments,
abandonment flexibility, and expansion options. These methods can be applied to
venture projects, R\&D investments, patent licensing, and infrastructure projects,
although the valuation is sensitive to assumptions.

Insurance companies use option-pricing methods to value some embedded guarantees
in annuities and life policies. Pension plans and insurers manage longevity and
other risks with a range of derivatives, including swaps and options. Exchanges
and clearinghouses use risk models for margin and settlement, while trading firms
may use Black--Scholes Greeks as one input in managing inventory risk.

Machine-learning methods are increasingly used alongside traditional models for
volatility forecasting, calibration, and trading research. Trading firms analyze
volatility surfaces and their dynamics, and Black--Scholes-style benchmarks are
also used for newer asset classes, including cryptocurrency options. The
Black--Scholes--Merton legacy continues to evolve as markets, technologies, and
risk-management practices change.
