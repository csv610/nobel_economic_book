\chapter{2013 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Eugene Fama (USA), Lars Peter Hansen (USA), Robert Shiller (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their empirical analysis of asset prices

\section{What They Did}
Eugene Fama, Lars Peter Hansen, and Robert Shiller advanced our understanding of
asset prices through complementary approaches that have shaped modern finance.
Fama studied informationally efficient markets, Hansen developed powerful
econometric methods for testing asset-pricing models, and Shiller documented
long-horizon predictability and behavioral explanations for price movements.

Fama's research on informationally efficient markets showed why stock prices are
very difficult to predict in the short run when new information is rapidly
incorporated into prices. His work, including joint research on size and value
effects, helped establish empirical methods for studying cross-sectional returns.

Hansen developed the generalized method of moments (GMM) and made foundational
contributions to the stochastic discount-factor approach, providing tools for
testing asset-pricing models. His 1982 paper introduced GMM, which became one of
the most important econometric methods in economics. The stochastic discount
factor provides a unifying language for expressing asset-pricing restrictions.

In consumption-based asset-pricing models, the Euler equation links asset prices
to consumption growth and investors' risk preferences. The consumption-based CAPM
relates expected returns to their covariance with consumption growth, providing a
theoretical framework for studying risk pricing.

Shiller documented excess volatility and long-horizon predictability in asset
prices, challenging simple versions of the efficient-market hypothesis with
evidence that prices fluctuate more than standard dividend-based models would
justify. He developed and popularized measures of stock-market valuation and
investigated behavioral explanations for price movements.

Shiller's CAPE ratio (cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio), developed
with John Campbell, has become a widely followed measure of long-run market
valuation. His work showed that stock prices exhibit excess volatility relative
to dividends, challenging simple models of fully rational and informationally
efficient pricing rather than proving that all market prices are irrational.

Together, they advanced empirical asset pricing through complementary methods:
Fama's tests of information in prices, Hansen's econometric tools, and Shiller's
evidence on predictability and behavioral explanations. Their findings do not
yield a single complete theory of asset pricing, but they define central issues
in the field.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Fama, Hansen, and Shiller transformed finance and empirical economics. Fama's
efficient-market hypothesis became a central paradigm, influencing investment
practice and financial research. His factor models provided tools for portfolio
management and performance evaluation.

The efficient-markets thesis reshaped how economists think about information and
price discovery. It influenced debates about active versus passive investing,
with the evidence suggesting that consistently outperforming broad benchmarks
after costs is difficult, especially for diversified investors.

Hansen's GMM and stochastic discount-factor approaches transformed econometric
testing, providing flexible methods for evaluating economic models. The Hansen--
Singleton consumption-based asset-pricing tests became influential in empirical
finance. His work showed how to test asset-pricing models using moment conditions
derived from economic theory.

Shiller's excess-volatility findings challenged simple versions of efficient
markets, sparking debates that enriched financial theory. His research helped
establish behavioral finance by examining how investor psychology and market
frictions can affect prices.

Their combined work established asset pricing as an empirical science, with
rigorous testing methods and theoretical foundations. The debate between
efficient markets and behavioral finance continues to drive research, with
evidence supporting both perspectives in different contexts.

The Nobel Prize recognized the complementary nature of their contributions:
Fama's empirical research, Hansen's methods, and Shiller's evidence on
predictability and valuation.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Asset-pricing theory built on their work remains central to finance and economics.
Factor models developed by Fama and French extend earlier work by incorporating
profitability and investment dimensions. The five-factor model organizes
cross-sectional return differences around market, size, value, profitability, and
investment factors, although no factor model explains every asset or period.

The stochastic discount-factor framework provides a unifying approach to thinking
about risk and expected returns. In principle, it can express pricing restrictions
for many assets, while practical derivative pricing also relies on no-arbitrage
arguments, model assumptions, and risk-neutral valuation.

Shiller's valuation metrics are widely followed by investors and policymakers.
The Shiller P/E ratio is a benchmark for long-run market valuation, but a high or
low reading does not by itself provide a precise timing signal or prove that a
market is overvalued or undervalued.

Behavioral finance, inspired in part by Shiller's work, has influenced research
and investment practice. The 2008 financial crisis renewed interest in asset-
pricing anomalies and debates about market efficiency.

Experimental and neuroeconomic research tests behavioral hypotheses about
investor decision-making. Climate risk and ESG investing create new asset
pricing challenges that build on their methodological foundations.

Their work continues to influence how economists understand market efficiency,
price formation, and the relationship between risk and return.
