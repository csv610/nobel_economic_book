\chapter{2010 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Peter Diamond (USA), Dale Mortensen (USA), Christopher Pissarides (UK/Cyprus)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their analysis of markets with search frictions

\section{What They Did}
Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen, and Christopher Pissarides developed search and
matching theory, including what became known as the DMP model, explaining how
markets function when buyers and sellers cannot find each other instantly.
Diamond was born in New York in 1940 and spent his career at MIT. Mortensen was
born in Enterprise, Oregon, in 1939 and spent most of his career at Northwestern
University. Pissarides was born in Nicosia, Cyprus, in 1948 and has been a
professor at the London School of Economics.

Their model showed that unemployment persists because finding suitable matches
takes time and resources. Job seekers search for vacancies, firms search for
workers, and the matching process determines the unemployment rate. This
framework provided microfoundations for unemployment that many earlier models
lacked, explaining why unemployment can exist even when wages are flexible.

Diamond made foundational contributions to search theory by studying how search
costs affect price formation. In a seminal 1971 paper, he showed that under its
assumptions even arbitrarily small search costs can lead firms to charge a common
monopoly price, a result known as the Diamond paradox. His work helped establish
the broader framework for analyzing search frictions in markets.

Mortensen contributed to equilibrium models of wage setting, showing how
bargaining between workers and firms can determine wages. He analyzed how
unemployment benefits affect search intensity and job acceptance rates,
demonstrating that, under standard assumptions, more generous benefits can
prolong unemployment by reducing the urgency to accept jobs. His work showed how
benefit design can affect equilibrium unemployment through job-search behavior.

Pissarides developed the matching function approach, quantifying how vacancies
and unemployment combine to produce hires. The commonly used Cobb--Douglas
matching function relates hires to the stocks of unemployed workers and vacant
jobs, with parameters capturing matching efficiency and the responsiveness of
hiring to each input. He analyzed how changes in matching efficiency affect
unemployment and labor-market dynamics.

Together, the three laureates created a framework for analyzing unemployment
dynamics, job creation and destruction, and the effectiveness of labor-market
policies. Their models explained why unemployment can persist even when wages
are flexible, because search and matching frictions prevent the market from
clearing immediately.

The Diamond--Mortensen--Pissarides (DMP) model has become a canonical framework
for analyzing labor market dynamics. It explains how job flows, unemployment
durations, and vacancy rates interact to determine the equilibrium unemployment
rate. The model has been extended to analyze on-the-job search, job destruction,
and the effects of labor market regulations.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Search and matching theory transformed labor economics by providing microfoundations
for unemployment. Before their work, unemployment was often explained by rigid
wages or exogenous shocks. The search framework showed that even with flexible
wages, unemployment can persist because workers and firms take time and resources
to find suitable matches.

This gave policymakers new tools for analyzing unemployment benefits, job search
requirements, and active labor market programs. The theory explained why
unemployment insurance affects search intensity and job acceptance rates,
informing debates about the appropriate level of benefits.

It also illuminated the determinants of vacancy creation and the efficiency of
labor market institutions. Business cycle fluctuations in unemployment could now
be understood through changes in matching efficiency and job destruction rates.
The framework explained why unemployment rises sharply during recessions.

The model showed that unemployment benefits create a tradeoff between insurance
and incentives. While benefits help workers smooth consumption during
unemployment, they can also reduce job-search effort and prolong spells of
unemployment under some conditions. This insight has informed debates about
benefit design and conditionality, including reforms that combine benefits with
job-search requirements.

The framework influenced macroeconomic modeling, with search frictions incorporated
into dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models. This helped broaden business-
cycle analysis beyond models with frictionless labor markets. Search-and-matching
models are also used to analyze how monetary policy affects labor-market outcomes,
although their use varies across central banks and research institutions.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Search and matching theory remains central to labor economics and macroeconomics.
Modern applications analyze unemployment dynamics during major downturns, the gig
economy's effects on job search, and the effects of automation on matching
efficiency.

Policymakers use matching models to evaluate unemployment benefit reforms, job
training programs, and employment subsidies. The framework has been extended to
analyze housing markets, credit markets, and platform economies where matching
frictions are prevalent.

Digital labor platforms like Uber and Upwork have created new matching
technologies that economists study using search theory. These platforms reduce
search costs but create new forms of friction, such as algorithmic management
and rating systems.

Research on wage inequality, job mobility, and career dynamics builds on the
Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides (DMP) framework. Studies of hysteresis--the idea
that unemployment spells can have long-lasting effects on earnings and employment--
extend the search framework to analyze scarring effects.

The theory also informs debates about minimum wage policies, unionization, and
worker protections. As labor markets evolve with technology and globalization,
search and matching theory provides essential analytical tools for understanding
unemployment and labor market dynamics.
