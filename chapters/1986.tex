\chapter{1986 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }James Buchanan (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For his development of the contractual and constitutional bases for the theory
of economic and political decision-making

\section{What They Did}
James Buchanan founded public choice theory applying economic principles to
political processes. He analyzed constitutional economics and government failure,
studying voting rules and collective action problems.

His framework treated politicians, bureaucrats, and voters as self-interested
actors operating within institutional constraints. His work showed democratic
processes produce inefficient outcomes and special interest capture.

Buchanan emphasized rules governing decision-making. He argued for choosing rules
before knowing outcomes, behind a veil of uncertainty. Constitutional political
economy examines fundamental rules constraining collective choice.

Buchanan and Gordon Tullock's "The Calculus of Consent" provided the theoretical
foundation for public choice theory. They analyzed conditions under which
collective action is possible and why democratic processes may fail to achieve
efficient outcomes.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Buchanan fundamentally altered understanding of government behavior. He showed
that democratic institutions are susceptible to the same self-interested
motivations as economic agents. Public choice theory revealed how politicians
seek votes and bureaus expand budgets.

Special interests capture regulations for rent extraction rather than public
benefit. His work exposed limitations of majority rule and revealed voting
paradoxes. Cyclical preferences showed why democratic processes may not yield
socially optimal outcomes.

Constitutional economics sought rules to constrain discretionary power and
prevent tyranny. Buchanan's analysis showed that institutions matter because
choices are constrained by chosen rules. Collective action problems explained
why inefficient policies persist despite widespread opposition.

The public choice revolution challenged the assumption that government
intervention automatically corrects market failures. Government failures can be
as costly as market failures.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Public choice theory remains indispensable for political economy, political
science, and government studies. Its analytical framework explains legislative
bargaining, campaign lobbying, and regulatory capture.

The rise of populism in advanced democracies has renewed interest in public
choice analysis. Buchanan's insights about benefit concentration and cost
dispersion explain populist policy persistence.

The role of special interests in shaping legislation and the influence of
campaign contributions illustrate Buchanan's prediction that government serves
organized interests. Contemporary research applies public choice tools to new
domains.

These include campaign finance regulation, judicial selection, central bank
independence, and international governance. The analysis of veto players,
political polarization, and institutional gridlock extends Buchanan's framework.
These explain policy stagnation in divided governments.
