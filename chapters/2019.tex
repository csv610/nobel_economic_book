\chapter{2019 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Abhijit Banerjee (India/USA), Esther Duflo (France/USA), Michael Kremer (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their experimental approach to alleviating global poverty

\section{What They Did}
Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo, and Michael Kremer helped establish an experiment-
based approach to studying poverty and development economics. Their use of
randomized controlled trials (RCTs), field experiments, and related methods has
transformed how economists evaluate development interventions and policy
effectiveness.

Banerjee and Duflo, who are married and colleagues at MIT, helped establish the
randomized-trial approach in development economics. They argued that instead of
debating only at the level of grand theories, economists should also test specific
interventions to learn what works in particular settings. Their approach has been
compared to randomized controlled trials in medical research. Together with
Sendhil Mullainathan, they founded the Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab
(J-PAL) at MIT, which has grown into a major international network of researchers
working to inform poverty policy with evidence.

Kremer, then at MIT, conducted pioneering field experiments in Kenya to test
educational and health interventions. His work on school inputs and deworming
helped demonstrate how carefully designed experiments can identify effects that
are otherwise difficult to separate from broader conditions. The deworming studies
also raised important questions about attendance, long-run effects, and spillovers
to nearby untreated children. His research on village-level public-goods provision
showed how community characteristics affect collective action.

Their collaborative work has produced extensive evidence on the effectiveness of various
development interventions, from education and health to microfinance and agriculture. They have
shown that many intuitive policy prescriptions do not work as expected, while some
counterintuitive interventions can be highly effective. For example, studies of
educational inputs in Kenya found that some interventions had effects that depended
strongly on students' prior preparation and local conditions.

Banerjee and Duflo's book ``Poor Economics'' (2011) synthesized their findings
and argued for a more nuanced approach to poverty alleviation. They showed that
poor households face complex tradeoffs and often make understandable decisions
given their constraints, challenging stereotypes about poor people's behavior.
Their research examined topics such as vaccination uptake, loan repayment, and
educational choices in developing countries.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Their work transformed development economics by emphasizing empirical evidence
alongside theory. RCTs have become an important tool for evaluating development
interventions, influencing policy debates and program design in many settings.

The emphasis on rigorous evidence has improved knowledge about which anti-poverty
programs work in particular settings and how resources might be allocated. Their
work has shown that small, targeted interventions can sometimes have large impacts,
while also revealing that effects vary across populations and institutions.

The experimental approach has also influenced other areas of economics, including
public economics, labor economics, and health economics. The emphasis on causal
inference and rigorous empirical methods has raised the standards of empirical
research across economics and strengthened evidence-based policymaking in
development.

Their work has influenced the design and evaluation of development programs, from
microfinance to education and health. J-PAL and related organizations have helped
governments and NGOs commission RCTs and use evidence when evaluating programs,
although translating results across settings remains difficult.

The emphasis on rigor and causal identification has influenced how economists think about
evidence more broadly. Their work has raised the bar for what counts as credible evidence
in development economics and has influenced policy debates about foreign aid, education,
health, and economic development worldwide.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
The experimental approach continues to expand to new areas of development
economics. Recent work by the broader research community has examined interventions
in health, education, and financial inclusion, as well as how poor households
responded to lockdowns and economic shocks during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The approach has been extended through field experiments and complemented by
natural-experiment and quasi-experimental methods. The emphasis on evidence-based
policy has influenced development practice, with governments and NGOs increasingly
commissioning RCTs to evaluate programs.

Their work has influenced a new generation of development economists who use
experimental methods to evaluate interventions. The emphasis on rigorous evidence
has changed how development economics is practiced and has influenced policy
debates about effective ways to alleviate poverty. The J-PAL network now connects
researchers and policy partners across universities and countries.

Recent work has examined the long-term effects of early-childhood interventions,
different teaching methods, and the impact of credit constraints on entrepreneurship.
The broader political economy literature also examines how voters respond to
policy interventions and how corruption affects program effectiveness.

The experimental approach has also been criticized for limitations, including
scaling successful interventions, external validity, ethical constraints, and
publication bias. Nevertheless, their work has fundamentally changed how
economists think about evidence and policy evaluation in development contexts.
