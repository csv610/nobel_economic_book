\chapter{2019 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Abhijit Banerjee (India/USA), Esther Duflo (France/USA), Michael Kremer (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their experimental approach to alleviating global poverty

\section{What They Did}
Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo, and Michael Kremer pioneered the use of randomized controlled
trials (RCTs) to study poverty and development economics. Their innovative methodology has
transformed how economists evaluate development interventions and policy effectiveness.

Banerjee and Duflo, who are married and colleagues at MIT, developed the randomized controlled
trial approach to development economics. They argued that instead of debating grand theories
about poverty, economists should test specific interventions to see what works. Their approach
has been compared to the randomized controlled trials used in medical research. They founded
the Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab (J-PAL) at MIT, which has grown to become the
world's largest network of researchers working to reduce poverty through evidence-based policy.

Kremer, also at MIT, conducted pioneering experiments in Kenya to test various educational
interventions, including class size reduction, teacher training, and deworming programs. His
work showed that small, targeted interventions could have large effects on educational outcomes
at low cost. He demonstrated that deworming children reduced school absenteeism significantly
and had positive spillover effects on nearby untreated children. His research on village-level
public goods provision showed how community characteristics affect collective action.

Their collaborative work has produced extensive evidence on the effectiveness of various
development interventions, from education and health to microfinance and agriculture. They have
shown that many intuitive policy prescriptions do not work as expected, while some
counterintuitive interventions can be highly effective. For example, they found that free
textbooks improved learning outcomes in Kenyan schools, but only when students were already
near grade level.

Banerjee and Duflo's book "Poor Economics" (2011) synthesized their findings and argued for
a more nuanced approach to poverty alleviation. They showed that the poor face complex tradeoffs
and make rational decisions given their constraints, challenging stereotypes about poor people's
behavior. Their research examined topics like vaccination uptake, loan repayment, and educational
choices in developing countries.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Their work transformed development economics by emphasizing empirical evidence over theoretical
speculation. The RCT methodology has become a standard tool for evaluating development
interventions, influencing policy debates and program design worldwide.

The emphasis on rigorous evidence has led to more effective anti-poverty programs and better
allocation of development resources. Their work has shown that small, targeted interventions
can have large impacts, challenging assumptions about the effectiveness of large-scale programs.
For example, they found that simple incentives like free desks improved learning outcomes
more than expensive infrastructure investments.

The experimental approach has also influenced other areas of economics, including public
economics, labor economics, and health economics. The emphasis on causal inference and
rigorous empirical methods has raised the standards of empirical research across economics.
Their work has spawned a movement toward evidence-based policy making in development.

Their work has influenced the design of development programs worldwide, from microfinance
programs to educational interventions. The evidence-based approach has led to more effective
use of development resources. J-PAL has influenced policy in dozens of countries, with
governments commissioning RCTs to evaluate their programs.

The emphasis on rigor and causal identification has influenced how economists think about
evidence more broadly. Their work has raised the bar for what counts as credible evidence
in development economics and has influenced policy debates about foreign aid, education,
health, and economic development worldwide.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
The experimental approach continues to expand to new areas of development economics. Recent
work has examined the effectiveness of various interventions in areas like health, education,
and financial inclusion. Their research on the COVID-19 pandemic has examined how poor
households responded to lockdowns and economic shocks.

The methodology has been extended to field experiments and natural experiments, applying
experimental methods to new contexts. The emphasis on evidence-based policy has influenced
development practice worldwide, with governments and NGOs increasingly commissioning RCTs
to evaluate their programs.

Their work has spawned a new generation of development economists who use experimental
methods to evaluate interventions. The emphasis on rigorous evidence has transformed how
development economics is practiced and has influenced policy debates about the most effective
ways to alleviate poverty. The J-PAL network has grown to include hundreds of researchers
at universities worldwide.

Recent work has examined the long-term effects of early childhood interventions, the
effectiveness of different teaching methods, and the impact of credit constraints on
entrepreneurship. Their research on the political economy of poverty has examined how
voters respond to policy interventions and how corruption affects program effectiveness.

The experimental approach has also been criticized for some limitations, including the
difficulty of scaling up successful interventions and the potential for publication bias.
Nevertheless, their work has fundamentally changed how economists think about evidence
and policy evaluation in development contexts.
