\chapter{2024 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Daron Acemoglu (Turkey/USA), Simon Johnson (UK/USA), James Robinson (UK/USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For their studies of how institutions are formed and affect prosperity

\section{What They Did}
Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson studied how institutions
shape economic development and prosperity. Their work has deepened our
understanding of why some nations become rich while others remain poor,
emphasizing the role of political and economic institutions while recognizing
that institutions interact with geography, culture, technology, and state
capacity.

They developed the concept of extractive versus inclusive institutions.
Extractive institutions concentrate power and wealth in the hands of a
small elite, extracting resources from the broader population. Inclusive
institutions distribute power more broadly and create incentives for
investment, innovation, and economic participation by the many. This
distinction provided a powerful framework for understanding cross-country
income differences.

Their influential book ``Why Nations Fail'' (2012) argued that institutional
differences are a major explanation for cross-country income differences, rather
than treating geography, culture, or policy knowledge as sufficient explanations
on their own. They traced how colonial institutions shaped modern economic
outcomes, showing how extractive regimes could create institutions that
persisted after independence. Their comparisons of societies formed under
different colonial strategies provided historical evidence for how political
power can shape economic institutions, although the trajectories of settler and
non-settler colonies were diverse rather than uniform.

They also examined the role of critical junctures--major historical events
like the Black Death, the Age of Discovery, and the Industrial Revolution--
that created opportunities for institutional change. The interaction between
critical junctures and existing institutions determined whether societies
moved toward more inclusive or more extractive paths. These historical
examples illustrated how differences in initial conditions and the distribution
of political power could lead to divergent development trajectories.

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson showed how institutional quality affects
investment incentives, innovation, and economic growth. Their work has
influenced policy debates about development, governance, and the role of
institutions in shaping economic outcomes. They argued that more inclusive
institutions can create stronger incentives for innovation and investment,
supporting long-run economic growth, but that such institutions are neither
sufficient for every development outcome nor easy to construct.

Their empirical work examined the relationship between institutions and
economic performance across countries and through history. They used historical
differences in colonial conditions, including settler mortality in some analyses,
to study the relationship between institutions and present-day economic outcomes.
These results provided evidence consistent with an important causal role for
institutions, while relying on assumptions that have been debated and do not
establish that geography or culture are irrelevant.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Their work reshaped development economics by placing political and economic
institutions at the centre of explanations for persistent prosperity gaps.
Earlier research had also considered geography, culture, human capital, and
policy, but the laureates showed how institutions shape incentives and how
political power can preserve or change them. This insight has influenced both
academic research and policy debates worldwide.

Their analysis of colonial history showed how initial institutional
choices have long-lasting effects. The contrast between colonial settings with
different settlement patterns, health conditions, and economic objectives
provides a framework for understanding how institutions can persist and affect
contemporary development. It is a historical tendency, not a universal
classification of every former colony.

Their work has influenced debates about democracy, governance, and economic
development. They showed how political and economic institutions can reinforce
one another: inclusive arrangements may broaden participation and support
prosperity, while extractive arrangements can give elites incentives to preserve
power. These feedbacks help explain why reform is difficult, but they do not
imply that every democracy is prosperous or every authoritarian regime is poor.
The framework has informed policy debates about democratization and governance
reform.

The framework has been applied to analyze institutional change, the role of
elite interests in blocking reform, and the conditions under which inclusive
institutions emerge. Their work has influenced policy debates about foreign
aid, governance reform, and the conditions for sustainable development.

Their research program integrates insights from history, political science, and
economics. The framework has been applied to questions about authoritarian
resilience, democratic backsliding, and the role of elites in blocking reform,
while leaving substantial room for country-specific analysis and competing
explanations.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
The institutional framework continues to inform research on economic
development, political economy, and the determinants of prosperity. Current
research examines how digital technologies affect institutions, how political
institutions shape responses to climate change, and how institutional choices
relate to inequality.

Their analysis provides one framework for debates about democratic backsliding,
the resilience of inclusive institutions, and authoritarian stability. It can
also help organize analysis of populism, nationalism, and strongman politics,
but these applications require evidence about the institutions and political
coalitions in each country.

Research on institutional quality continues to inform policy debates about
development assistance, governance reform, and international institutions.
Their work has encouraged attention to the political constraints on reform in
countries with extractive legacies. The concept of inclusive institutions is
also used to analyze corruption, the rule of law, and democratic governance,
though institutional reform cannot be reduced to a single policy template.

The institutional approach has also been extended to analyze the economics
of corruption, the role of social norms in supporting institutions, and the
interactions between institutions and economic behavior. Their research program
continues to generate work on the foundations of economic prosperity. Related
research examines how institutions shape the distributional effects of
artificial intelligence and other technological changes, including whether new
technologies broaden opportunity or reinforce existing concentrations of power.
