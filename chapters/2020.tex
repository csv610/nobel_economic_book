\chapter{2020 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Paul Milgrom (USA), Robert Wilson (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For improvements to auction theory and inventions of new auction formats

\section{What They Did}
Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson advanced auction theory and helped design new
auction formats that have been used around the world. Their work combined
theoretical insights with practical design to improve allocation and information
in complex auctions; the appropriate objective may be efficiency, revenue, or a
broader public benefit, depending on the setting.

Wilson developed the theory of common value auctions, where the item being sold has the same
value to all bidders but bidders receive different signals about that value. He analyzed the
winner's curse--the tendency for the winner of a common value auction to overpay because they
had the most optimistic signal. Wilson showed why bidders should shade their bids
to account for the winner's curse and developed foundational models of behavior in
common-value settings. These models also clarified how information and strategic
interaction affect auction outcomes.

Milgrom and later collaborators developed theory and formats for combinatorial
auctions, where bidders can bid on packages of items rather than individual
items. This is important when items are complements and the value of a package
exceeds the sum of individual values. Such designs can improve allocation, but
their performance depends on computational, informational, and strategic
conditions.

Milgrom, Wilson, and collaborators helped design the simultaneous multiple-round
ascending (SMRA) auction used by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) to auction
radio-spectrum licenses. The format was first used by the FCC in 1994 and was
later adapted in other countries. Milgrom and collaborators also developed the
combinatorial clock auction and the incentive auction, which allow auction rules
to reflect complementarities and complex public-policy objectives.

Milgrom's earlier work on auction theory analyzed bidding strategies, affiliated
values, and how information revealed during bidding affects bidder behavior and
seller revenue. His work helped clarify when different auction formats produce
different outcomes and how dynamic rules can be designed.

Wilson also contributed to the analysis of private information and trading
processes. Together with Milgrom and Weber's broader research program, these
ideas clarified how correlated signals affect equilibrium bidding strategies and
auction outcomes.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Their work transformed auction theory from an abstract mathematical discipline
into a practical tool for designing markets. The theoretical insights they
developed have been applied to auction contexts ranging from spectrum and
electricity markets to online advertising.

The practical applications of their work have been extensive. Spectrum auctions
designed using related insights have generated substantial public revenue in many
countries, while their work has also influenced auctions for emissions permits,
procurement contracts, and other public resources.

The theoretical framework they developed has become standard in auction theory and
mechanism design. Their insights about the winner's curse, complementary values,
and information in auctions have been widely applied in academic research and
practical design. Common-value models are used to analyze bidding in oil and gas
leases, mineral rights, and other natural-resource auctions.

Wilson's analysis of the winner's curse has influenced how companies approach
bidding in acquisition contexts and other common-value settings. Milgrom's work
and related auction research have also informed online advertising platforms,
where automated auctions allocate advertising opportunities.

Their work demonstrated that auction design matters for economic outcomes. By
carefully structuring auction rules, designers can better align outcomes with the
goals of sellers, buyers, and the public. This insight has been applied to settings
including spectrum allocation, electricity markets, airline slots, and procurement.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Auction theory based on Milgrom and Wilson's work continues to evolve, with new
applications to online advertising, electricity markets, and other contexts. The
combinatorial clock auction has been used in some spectrum-auction settings.

The theoretical framework continues to be extended to new contexts, including
dynamic auctions, network industries, and platform markets. Their work remains
foundational for understanding how auction rules affect allocation, information,
and incentives. Research on algorithmic auction design has been particularly
important for digital platforms.

The practical applications of auction theory continue to expand, with new formats
designed for settings such as carbon-emissions trading and bandwidth allocation.
The FCC spectrum auctions helped demonstrate how carefully designed formats can
be adapted internationally, although each market requires its own rules and
institutional safeguards.

Recent work has extended auction theory to bidders with budget constraints,
risk aversion, and heterogeneous values. These extensions matter for settings
including public procurement, spectrum allocation, internet advertising, and other
digital markets.
