\chapter{2002 Nobel Prize in Economics}
\textbf{Laureates: }Daniel Kahneman (Israel/USA), Vernon Smith (USA)

\section*{Reason for Award}
For having integrated insights from psychological research into economic science,
especially concerning human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty

\section{What They Did}
Kahneman and Smith transformed economics by incorporating psychological
realism into economic analysis, challenging the standard model of the rational
economic agent that had dominated the discipline for decades. Kahneman, born
in Tel Aviv in 1934, brought insights from cognitive psychology to economics.
Smith, born in Wichita, Kansas, in 1927, established experimental methods for
testing economic theories.

Kahneman, together with the late Amos Tversky, developed prospect theory,
which describes how people actually make decisions under risk and uncertainty.
Unlike expected utility theory, which assumes that individuals evaluate
outcomes based on final wealth positions and maximize expected utility,
prospect theory shows that people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference
point--typically the status quo. Losses relative to the reference point are
weighted more heavily than equivalent gains, a phenomenon known as loss
aversion. Many experiments find loss aversion, but its magnitude varies with the
reference point, framing, task, and population; a frequently reported estimate
is that losses receive roughly twice the weight of equivalent gains.

Kahneman and Tversky identified a range of cognitive heuristics and biases
that affect judgment under uncertainty. The availability heuristic leads
people to judge the probability of events based on how easily examples come
to mind. The representativeness heuristic leads people to judge probabilities
based on similarity to stereotypes, ignoring base rates and sample sizes. The
anchoring heuristic causes people to rely too heavily on initial information
when making decisions.

Smith helped establish experimental economics as a rigorous method for testing
economic theories. Earlier economists often regarded economics as a largely
non-experimental science. In pioneering double-auction experiments, Smith showed
that prices could converge rapidly toward competitive-equilibrium predictions
even when participants lacked the information and calculations needed to derive
those prices. The results supported the predictive value of market institutions
under particular conditions, while later experiments also revealed systematic
deviations and institutional effects.

Smith also showed how market institutions and trading rules shape economic
outcomes. His work on experimental methodology provided tools for testing
economic theories under controlled
conditions, revealing patterns of behavior that challenged standard assumptions.

\section{Impact on Economic Thinking}
Kahneman's behavioral revolution challenged the rational agent paradigm that
had dominated economics since the neoclassical revolution. Prospect theory
provided a descriptive alternative to expected-utility theory that has been used
to analyze phenomena such as the disposition effect in trading and the endowment
effect. Later research has applied related behavioral ideas to asset pricing,
retirement saving, and other economic decisions, but these applications are not
direct results of the original theory alone.

Smith's experimental methods transformed economics from a discipline that
relied primarily on theoretical deduction and observational data into one
that could test hypotheses under controlled conditions. Experimental economics
became a recognized subfield with its own journals, professional associations,
and methodological standards. The field now includes laboratory experiments,
field experiments, and natural experiments.

The interaction of behavioral insights and experimental methods contributed to new
interdisciplinary research programs. Behavioral game theory incorporated
psychological realism into strategic analysis, relaxing the assumption of
common knowledge of rationality. Neuroeconomics investigated the neural
correlates of decision-making using tools like functional magnetic resonance
imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG).

Kahneman's distinction between the experiencing self and the remembering self,
developed in later work with collaborators, raised important questions about
welfare economics and the measurement of well-being. His research also influenced
later work on choice architecture in organizations and government agencies.

Smith's experimental approach influenced the development of behavioral
game theory and the study of social preferences. Experimental methods
have been applied to study fairness, reciprocity, and altruism, revealing
that people care about outcomes beyond their own material payoffs.

\section{Modern Perspectives Today}
Kahneman and Smith's legacy is evident throughout contemporary economics and
public policy. Nudge theory, popularized by Thaler and Sunstein, applies
behavioral insights to policy design through choice architecture--altering the
way choices are presented to influence behavior without restricting freedom of
choice. Governments worldwide have established behavioral insight units to
improve policy outcomes in areas ranging from tax collection and organ donation
to retirement savings and energy conservation.

Experimental economics has expanded beyond laboratory settings, while field
experiments and natural experiments also developed through partly independent
traditions. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) now inform development economics,
education policy, healthcare delivery, and environmental policy. Smith's work
helped legitimize controlled experimentation in economics, but the modern RCT
movement has multiple intellectual and disciplinary roots.

Behavioral economics has influenced financial regulation, consumer protection,
and retirement-savings policy. Its growth as a research field reflects broad
contributions from psychologists and economists, including Kahneman and Tversky.
Kahneman's and Smith's distinct contributions remain central to understanding
how people make decisions and how market institutions perform.

The integration of psychology into economics has enriched the discipline by
providing more realistic models of human behavior. Modern economic theory
incorporates bounded rationality, social preferences, and cognitive biases,
while experimental methods test how people and markets behave under specified
conditions. Their work helped broaden economics beyond a single idealized model
of decision-making.
